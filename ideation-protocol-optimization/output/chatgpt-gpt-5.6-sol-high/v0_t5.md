## Generate ideas

Assume the city cannot rely on road widening, generic “improve public transport,” carpool campaigns, more parking, or standard congestion pricing.

1. **School-start staggering**
   Assign schools in the same district different start times, reducing the sharp morning peak created by school trips.

2. **Freight delivery windows as tradable slots**
   Give businesses limited peak-hour delivery permits. Unused permits can be sold, encouraging consolidation without banning deliveries.

3. **Traffic-light auctions for buses and high-occupancy vehicles**
   Vehicles carrying more people receive stronger signal priority. Road capacity is allocated by passengers moved, not vehicles moved.

4. **Employer peak-hour budgets**
   Large employers receive a monthly allowance for employee arrivals during peak periods. They can use it through remote work, staggered shifts, satellite offices, or pay for excess demand.

5. **“No simultaneous disruption” rule**
   Coordinate construction, school events, stadium events, utility work, and road closures so multiple disruptions cannot overlap within the same traffic zone.

6. **Neighborhood mobility depots**
   Convert selected parking lots into transfer points for shared bicycles, small electric shuttles, parcel lockers, taxis, and remote-work booths. People complete only the final segment locally.

7. **Dynamic curb allocation**
   Curbs change function by time: deliveries at dawn, bus lanes during peaks, pickup zones at midday, dining or parking at night.

8. **Reverse parking incentives**
   Charge less for parking when drivers arrive outside peak periods and more when they enter during the peak, even if total parking duration is identical.

9. **Guaranteed ride home**
   Employers or the city guarantee an emergency taxi ride for commuters who normally avoid driving. This removes a major psychological barrier to using transit, cycling, or carpooling.

10. **Appointment-time coordination**
    Hospitals, government offices, universities, and large service businesses share scheduling data and avoid concentrating appointments at the same times.

11. **Road-space subscriptions**
    Frequent drivers buy access to a limited number of peak-hour road entries per month, similar to mobile-data plans. Occasional urgent trips remain possible.

12. **Traffic evaporation experiments**
    Temporarily close one troublesome junction or corridor for several weeks rather than assuming displaced traffic will remain constant. Some trips may change time, route, destination, or disappear.

13. **Commuter exchange**
    Match workers living near one another who commute in opposite directions and facilitate job-location swaps between branches of the same company or public agency.

14. **Portable offices on commuter routes**
    Place reservable work pods near rail stations and residential centers, allowing workers to complete the first two hours of work locally and travel after the peak.

15. **Personal congestion forecasts**
    Navigation apps show not only journey time but the delay a trip imposes on everyone else. Employers or insurers reward users who choose low-impact departure times.

16. **Event departure choreography**
    Stadiums, malls, and concert venues release parking areas in timed waves, with discounts, entertainment, or food credits for people who leave later.

17. **Parcel consolidation mandate**
    Large buildings receive deliveries through a single logistics operator or shared loading hub, reducing multiple courier trips to the same address.

18. **Artificial midweek weekend**
    Different sectors receive different low-commute weekdays: finance on Friday, education on Wednesday, government on Monday. Demand is spread across the week rather than merely across the day.

## Ban the obvious

Discard road expansion because induced demand can refill capacity. Discard generic awareness campaigns because convenience usually dominates persuasion. Discard blanket work-from-home mandates because they are inflexible and politically fragile. Discard free public transport alone because fare price is often not the main constraint. Discard citywide driving bans because enforcement and equity problems are severe.

## Best practical idea

**Dynamic curb allocation combined with a no-simultaneous-disruption rule.**

It uses infrastructure the city already owns, can be piloted corridor by corridor, and addresses two neglected causes of congestion: badly allocated curb space and poorly coordinated disruptions. A city could begin with ten congested corridors, use cameras or sensors for enforcement, and change curb rules by time of day.

## Best wildcard idea

**Employer peak-hour budgets.**

Instead of charging every driver, the city targets organizations that generate concentrated demand. Each major employer receives a limited number of peak-period arrival credits. Employers decide how to comply: staggered shifts, remote mornings, branch transfers, commuter shuttles, or purchasing extra credits. It converts congestion from an individual moral problem into an operational scheduling problem that institutions are better equipped to solve.
