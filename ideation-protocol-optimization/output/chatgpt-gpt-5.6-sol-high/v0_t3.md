## Core thesis

**Physical bookstores are not dying; undifferentiated book warehouses are.** In 2025, the American Booksellers Association reported 19% membership growth, 605 new store openings, and higher year-over-year sales among 73.3% of surveyed stores. Barnes & Noble’s recovery similarly relied on local curation, store-level authority, smaller formats, and fewer inventory returns—not matching Amazon on price or selection. Younger adults also show relatively strong preference for buying books in-store. ([the American Booksellers Association][1])

The chain should stop selling access to books and start selling **belonging, discovery, and intellectual progress**.

## 1. Generate ideas

1. Six-week “reading seasons” combining books, facilitated discussions and a final experience.
2. A paid reader membership: program credits, quiet-space access, exchanges and concierge recommendations.
3. Corporate book clubs sold as leadership-development programs.
4. Family memberships with children’s cohorts and parent programming.
5. Book-concierge appointments, similar to personal shopping.
6. Reader-to-reader matching based on literary taste.
7. A “finish it or exchange it” guarantee.
8. Used-book trade-ins that create a circular, lower-priced inventory layer.
9. Community members operating clubs under a revenue-sharing model.
10. Local publishing studios offering editing, design and print-on-demand.
11. Each location specializing in subjects important to its neighbourhood.
12. Rotating “exhibition shelves” built around questions rather than genres.
13. Laptop-free evening reading salons.
14. Mobile bookstore clubs inside offices, schools and residential communities.
15. Author residencies funded by members.
16. Literary city walks, story trails and reading retreats.
17. “Book as ticket”: purchasing the book includes admission to the related program.
18. Reader credentials—for example, completing a ten-book climate, history or leadership pathway.
19. Pre-sell a community before opening or renewing a store lease.
20. Build a proprietary human recommendation network across the chain.

## 2. Ban the obvious

Do not make the strategy:

* Price matching Amazon.
* A larger online catalogue.
* Another generic loyalty-points program.
* Adding a café and hoping people buy books.
* Free author talks and book clubs treated as marketing expenses.
* More stationery, gifts and toys.
* Identical inventory across every location.
* Blanket discounting or indiscriminate cost cutting.
* An expensive app before proving that customers value the underlying service.

These may support the strategy, but none creates a defensible reason to visit regularly.

## 3. Chase the unlikely

### Sell outcomes, not objects

A customer should not buy *Atomic Habits*. They should join a six-week “Build Better Habits” cohort whose materials include the book.

Books become the curriculum. The store sells progress, accountability and companionship.

### Turn customers into operators

Recruit skilled customers to run philosophy circles, children’s storytelling, writing workshops or professional-learning groups. Give hosts training, space, promotion and 25–35% of program revenue.

The chain gains hundreds of credible programmers without carrying hundreds of full-time salaries.

### Treat silence as a premium product

Some locations should offer evening “reading rooms”: device-free, conversation-light spaces with reserved seats, drinks and facilitated reading intervals.

The scarce resource being sold is not coffee or floor space. It is protected attention.

### Become a local publisher

Use events and membership data to identify unmet demand, then commission neighbourhood histories, local guides, children’s books and specialist short editions. Print in small batches or on demand.

Instead of merely accepting publishers’ inventory risk, the chain owns differentiated intellectual property.

### Build a human alternative to recommendation algorithms

Capture each reader’s “taste fingerprint”: ten loved books, five abandoned books, desired emotional experiences and trusted booksellers.

Recommendations would say:

> “Chosen by Maya from the Riverside store, whose taste overlaps yours on seven of ten books.”

The defensible asset is not the algorithm. It is the network of trusted humans behind it.

## Best practical idea: the Reader House Network

Transform every store from a retail outlet into a locally programmed **Reader House**.

Each location owns four to six recurring communities—for example:

* Young families.
* Crime and speculative-fiction readers.
* Professionals interested in leadership and technology.
* Teen readers and creators.
* History and public-affairs readers.

Each community receives:

* A recurring facilitated gathering.
* Curated books and reading pathways.
* A dedicated staff curator.
* Member-led sessions.
* Priority access to visiting authors.
* Exchanges and personal recommendations.
* Access across every branch in the city.

Offer three commercial layers:

1. **Free discovery:** browsing, basic recommendations and occasional public events.
2. **Membership:** monthly credits, exchange privileges, reading-room access and member pricing.
3. **Paid journeys:** four-to-eight-week programs bundled with books and facilitation.

The same programs can be sold to employers, schools, libraries and residential communities. Online commerce remains necessary, but primarily as the fulfilment layer for the relationship: click-and-collect, replenishment, digital books and delivery. Independent-store online sales can complement rather than replace physical engagement; ABA’s IndieCommerce stores have demonstrated substantial online growth. ([the American Booksellers Association][2])

### Target year-three revenue mix

| Revenue source                            | Target |
| ----------------------------------------- | -----: |
| Books                                     | 55–65% |
| Memberships and consumer programs         | 15–20% |
| Corporate and school contracts            | 10–15% |
| Used books, publishing and other services |    10% |

The objective is not necessarily to reduce book revenue. It is to make book purchasing the consequence of a recurring relationship rather than a one-off transaction.

## Wildcard: the Human Taste Graph

Create the first chain-wide recommendation system built around identifiable human taste rather than anonymous star ratings.

A member could:

* Find booksellers and readers with similar taste.
* Request three selections from a particular curator.
* Subscribe to that person’s monthly shelf.
* Meet nearby readers with compatible interests.
* Follow recommendation trails: “A recommended B, who recommended C.”
* Send a “literary prescription” as a gift.

Start manually using customer profiles and existing CRM software. Do not build sophisticated technology until the chain has at least several thousand active profiles and evidence that recommendations convert.

Over time, this becomes a proprietary asset that online retailers cannot easily reproduce: **trusted relationships tied to local places and recognisable people**.

The main risk is corrupting trust through paid recommendations. Publisher sponsorship should therefore be disclosed and kept outside the core matching system.

## Three-year execution

### Year 1: Prove the new unit

Select four contrasting pilot stores. Give each manager authority over at least half the assortment, displays and programming.

Launch:

* Three paid reading journeys per store.
* One simple membership.
* Human taste profiles.
* Community-host revenue sharing.
* One corporate or school offering.

Stop measuring events by attendance alone. Measure revenue, attached book sales, repeat visits and subsequent membership.

Kill or redesign programs that cannot achieve 70% capacity or produce meaningful downstream sales after three iterations.

### Year 2: Build the network

Expand the model to the strongest half of the estate.

Centralise technology, procurement, finance and program templates. Decentralise curation, partnerships and community programming.

Train staff as curators and facilitators rather than merely cashiers and shelf stockers. Create citywide membership, cross-store access and portable reader profiles.

Launch corporate and school sales teams. Turn successful local programs into replicable formats that other stores can license from the originating host.

### Year 3: Reshape the estate

Convert successful locations fully into Reader Houses.

Resize, relocate or close stores that cannot form viable communities. Replace some large stores with smaller neighbourhood clubhouses, pop-ups or mobile formats.

Launch the Human Taste Graph and local publishing selectively. Open new stores only after pre-selling sufficient memberships, institutional contracts or community-host commitments.

## The year-three scorecard

The strategy is working when:

* 15–25% of gross profit comes from recurring or contracted revenue.
* Members visit at least twice as frequently as non-members.
* Twelve-month membership retention exceeds 65%.
* Paid-program occupancy exceeds 70%.
* Inventory turns improve by at least 25%.
* Returns to publishers fall materially.
* More than half of store programming is led or co-led by community hosts.
* Each store has a recognisable local identity.
* New locations can recruit a community before signing a long lease.

The decisive shift is:

**From stores containing the same books to a network containing different people.**

Amazon can deliver the next book. The chain must own the reason someone reads it, the people they discuss it with and the identity they build around it.
