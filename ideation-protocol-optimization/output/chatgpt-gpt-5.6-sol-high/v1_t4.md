## IDEATE

### 1. Five perspectives

#### A. A blind audiobook power user

1. **Tactile Syntax** — Texture, pressure and shape encode voice, emphasis, chronology and cross-references rather than merely reproducing printed letters.
2. **Spatial Narrative Architecture** — Characters and ideas occupy stable acoustic locations around the reader; movement represents relationships and transitions.
3. **Recall Dialogue** — The book periodically asks the reader to retell what happened, then continues using the reader’s own vocabulary and omissions.

#### B. A game designer

4. **Commitment Edition** — The reader must commit to a theory, moral position or prediction; subsequent evidence is presented differently because of that commitment.
5. **Boss-Key Chapters** — Progress requires demonstrating a usable mental model, not answering factual questions.
6. **Persistent-World Book** — Ideas become objects in a simulated environment; understanding a chapter changes how those objects behave.

#### C. A cognitive scientist

7. **Prediction-First Book** — Readers predict the next argument, event or explanation before seeing it.
8. **Spaced-Echo Edition** — Important ideas return days later in altered forms, making the book a scheduled experience rather than a continuous object.
9. **Memory-Palace Edition** — The book attaches sections to places in the reader’s home, commute or neighbourhood.

#### D. A choreographer

10. **Somatic Score** — Passages are experienced through instructed movements, posture, breathing or coordination.
11. **Duet Edition** — Two readers receive complementary fragments and must synchronise speech or action to reconstruct the work.
12. **Role-Switch Reading** — Readers repeatedly change physical and interpretive roles: witness, participant, antagonist, observer.

#### E. An investigative journalist

13. **Contradiction Dossier** — The book consists of conflicting testimony, records and omissions; the reader reconstructs the underlying story.
14. **Confidence-Layered Book** — Every claim visibly carries its level and type of uncertainty.
15. **Evidence-Harvest Book** — The reader must collect observations, conversations, photographs or measurements from real life to complete the text.

### 2. Borrowing structures from unrelated domains

## Domain A: Molecular gastronomy

### Atomic rule 1: Deconstruct familiar objects into components, then recombine them

16. **Tasting-Menu Book** — The work is organised into provocations, proofs, applications, counterpoints and aftertastes rather than chapters.
17. **Concept Pairing Edition** — Distant passages are deliberately paired because their ideas interact, much as unexpected flavours do.

### Atomic rule 2: Control when an ingredient is released

18. **Timed-Release Book** — Sentences, explanations or alternative endings become available hours, weeks or years later.
19. **Context-Key Capsules** — Passages unlock only after the reader has encountered a relevant place, event, emotion or activity.

### Atomic rule 3: Contrast intensifies perception

20. **Temperature-Shifting Edition** — The same material is presented under sharply different emotional or rhetorical framings.
21. **Palate-Cleanser Interludes** — Deliberate silence, unrelated sensory exercises or empty pages reset attention between dense sections.

## Domain B: Cryptographic protocols

### Atomic rule 1: Information is revealed only when the correct proof or key is supplied

22. **Zero-Knowledge Book** — Readers unlock material by demonstrating understanding without reproducing the author’s explanation.
23. **Personal-Key Edition** — Parts of the book can be decoded only using facts from the reader’s own memories and experiences.

### Atomic rule 2: A secret can be distributed so no participant possesses the whole

24. **Secret-Sharing Edition** — Different readers receive incomplete but complementary versions.
25. **Intergenerational Relay Book** — Each generation holds part of the interpretive key and passes an altered edition onward.

### Atomic rule 3: Consensus and tamper evidence create trust

26. **Consensus Chapter** — A group can proceed only after recording an interpretation everyone accepts, including unresolved disagreements.
27. **Palimpsest Ledger** — Every rereading transforms the visible edition while preserving a traceable history of earlier interpretations.

---

### 3. Conventional ideas banned

These five familiar approaches, including near-duplicates, are excluded:

1. Ordinary, dramatised or spatial audiobooks.
2. Choose-your-own-adventure branching.
3. Quizzes, badges, streaks and conventional gamification.
4. Video, animation, augmented reality or generic multimedia overlays.
5. Book-club discussion and shared marginal annotations.

Spatial sound, branching decisions, tests and social interaction may appear only when they produce a fundamentally different reading mechanism—not merely a decorated ebook.

---

### 4. Merged surviving set

Overlapping candidates reduce to these 17 distinct formats:

1. **Prediction-First Book** — Reading begins with constructing an expectation.
2. **Recall Mirror** — The book periodically reflects the reader’s reconstruction back to them.
3. **Timed-Release Book** — Parts of the work unfold according to elapsed time.
4. **Context-Key Capsules** — Real-world circumstances determine when passages become readable.
5. **Zero-Knowledge Book** — Progress requires evidence of understanding rather than correct answers.
6. **Commitment Edition** — Early interpretations change later access and framing.
7. **Secret-Sharing Edition** — No individual receives the complete work.
8. **Consensus Chapter** — Collective interpretation becomes part of the reading protocol.
9. **Contradiction Dossier** — The reader creates the coherent account from incompatible evidence.
10. **Somatic Score** — Movement and bodily state carry part of the meaning.
11. **Role-Mask Edition** — Readers receive information according to temporary roles.
12. **Memory-Palace Route** — The reader’s physical environment becomes the book’s structure.
13. **Tasting-Menu Book** — Ideas are consumed in deliberately contrasting conceptual courses.
14. **Evidence-Harvest Book** — Reality supplies missing material.
15. **Intergenerational Relay Book** — The reading unfolds across years and readers.
16. **Tactile Syntax** — Material texture functions as literary grammar.
17. **Palimpsest Ledger** — Rereading modifies the edition without erasing its prior forms.

## CONVERGE

Scores are out of five. “Speed” means speed to a credible prototype.

### 1. Prediction-First Book

**Assumption:** Active prediction materially changes attention and retention without becoming tiresome.

**Why non-obvious:** It makes the reader’s wrong model part of the text. Surprise becomes visible as the distance between prediction and revelation.

**Why it may fail:** Frequent interruptions could destroy narrative flow, especially in fiction.

**Scores:** Impact 5 · Novelty 4 · Speed 5

### 2. Recall Mirror

**Assumption:** Readers will tolerate having their misunderstandings and selective memories exposed.

**Why non-obvious:** The reader’s remembered version becomes a parallel edition, sometimes more revealing than the author’s version.

**Why it may fail:** AI-generated reflection could feel evaluative, intrusive or inaccurate.

**Scores:** Impact 5 · Novelty 4 · Speed 4

### 3. Timed-Release Book

**Assumption:** Waiting increases meaning rather than merely frustrating the reader.

**Why non-obvious:** Time becomes punctuation. A ten-day pause can perform the function of a paragraph break.

**Why it may fail:** Readers may abandon the work or manipulate device clocks.

**Scores:** Impact 3 · Novelty 4 · Speed 5

### 4. Context-Key Capsules

**Assumption:** Relevant moments in a reader’s life can be detected or self-reported accurately.

**Why non-obvious:** The book is no longer located in pages; it appears when the reader’s life supplies the correct interpretive context.

**Why it may fail:** Privacy concerns, unreliable sensing and unwanted interruptions could overwhelm the literary effect.

**Scores:** Impact 4 · Novelty 5 · Speed 3

### 5. Zero-Knowledge Book

**Assumption:** Understanding can be demonstrated through novel applications without reducing reading to a test.

**Why non-obvious:** The reader need not state the author’s idea. They prove possession of it by successfully using it elsewhere.

**Why it may fail:** Designing valid proofs for subjective or literary material is difficult.

**Scores:** Impact 4 · Novelty 5 · Speed 3

### 6. Commitment Edition

**Assumption:** Temporarily restricting alternative interpretations deepens reflection.

**Why non-obvious:** Most books preserve reader freedom. This format uses irreversible commitment to expose bias and motivated reasoning.

**Why it may fail:** Readers may resent hidden content or make arbitrary choices merely to continue.

**Scores:** Impact 4 · Novelty 5 · Speed 4

### 7. Secret-Sharing Edition

**Assumption:** Readers will coordinate despite knowing that each has an incomplete and potentially misleading text.

**Why non-obvious:** Incompleteness is not a defect but the central literary mechanism. Other people become necessary reading instruments.

**Why it may fail:** Scheduling and participation friction may make the work inaccessible.

**Scores:** Impact 4 · Novelty 5 · Speed 3

### 8. Consensus Chapter

**Assumption:** The negotiation required to agree on meaning is itself valuable.

**Why non-obvious:** Interpretation becomes an authored event. Disagreement is part of the book’s formal structure rather than commentary after reading.

**Why it may fail:** Dominant participants may force superficial agreement; groups may stall.

**Scores:** Impact 3 · Novelty 5 · Speed 3

### 9. Contradiction Dossier

**Assumption:** Readers enjoy constructing a model from incomplete, biased evidence.

**Why non-obvious:** The author never provides a privileged narrative voice. Credibility assessment replaces ordinary exposition.

**Why it may fail:** Ambiguity may be mistaken for poor explanation, particularly in educational books.

**Scores:** Impact 5 · Novelty 4 · Speed 4

### 10. Somatic Score

**Assumption:** Bodily action can carry conceptual or narrative meaning that prose alone cannot.

**Why non-obvious:** Reading becomes closer to performing sheet music than decoding language.

**Why it may fail:** It excludes some readers unless movements are adaptable, and it may feel theatrical or embarrassing.

**Scores:** Impact 3 · Novelty 5 · Speed 2

### 11. Role-Mask Edition

**Assumption:** Selective access produces genuine perspective-taking rather than mere information asymmetry.

**Why non-obvious:** A character’s ignorance is imposed directly on the reader. Perspective becomes an access-control system.

**Why it may fail:** Readers may circumvent restrictions or become confused about what is canonically true.

**Scores:** Impact 4 · Novelty 5 · Speed 3

### 12. Memory-Palace Route

**Assumption:** Readers are willing to map a work onto familiar physical locations.

**Why non-obvious:** Navigation through one’s home, office or neighbourhood becomes navigation through the argument.

**Why it may fail:** The setup burden is high, and changing environments can break the structure.

**Scores:** Impact 4 · Novelty 4 · Speed 3

### 13. Tasting-Menu Book

**Assumption:** Conceptual sequencing can be redesigned without losing coherence.

**Why non-obvious:** A book need not move from beginning to end or from summary to detail. It can manage intellectual appetite through contrast, intensity and aftertaste.

**Why it may fail:** The metaphor may produce clever packaging without changing comprehension.

**Scores:** Impact 4 · Novelty 3 · Speed 5

### 14. Evidence-Harvest Book

**Assumption:** Readers will perform real-world actions to obtain missing pieces.

**Why non-obvious:** The environment becomes a co-author. The book deliberately leaves holes that cannot be filled by more text.

**Why it may fail:** Results vary by location, privilege, mobility and willingness to approach other people.

**Scores:** Impact 5 · Novelty 5 · Speed 3

### 15. Intergenerational Relay Book

**Assumption:** Readers value an experience they cannot personally complete.

**Why non-obvious:** Completion is designed to exceed an individual lifespan or life stage. Stewardship replaces consumption.

**Why it may fail:** Physical and digital continuity over decades is difficult; later participants may feel no obligation to continue.

**Scores:** Impact 4 · Novelty 5 · Speed 2

### 16. Tactile Syntax

**Assumption:** Readers can learn a material grammar without excessive cognitive load.

**Why non-obvious:** Texture is treated not as accessibility metadata or decoration but as a semantic channel comparable to tense or punctuation.

**Why it may fail:** Manufacturing is expensive, conventions are unfamiliar and digital distribution is limited.

**Scores:** Impact 4 · Novelty 5 · Speed 2

### 17. Palimpsest Ledger

**Assumption:** Readers value accumulated interpretive history more than a clean, stable edition.

**Why non-obvious:** The book is not the original text alone. It is the sequence of relationships that readers have had with it.

**Why it may fail:** Layers may become cluttered, self-conscious or dominated by earlier readers.

**Scores:** Impact 4 · Novelty 5 · Speed 3

## Best practical ideas

### 1. Prediction-First Book

**Non-obvious insight:** A reader’s expectation is unwritten text. Capturing it makes surprise, learning and conceptual change observable.

A prototype requires only carefully placed prediction prompts and delayed reveals. It could work immediately for mysteries, textbooks, business books and popular science.

### 2. Contradiction Dossier

**Non-obvious insight:** Explanation can be replaced by evidence arbitration. The act of deciding whom to trust can teach more than a polished narrative.

This is particularly promising for history, journalism, ethics, management cases and scientific controversies.

### 3. Recall Mirror

**Non-obvious insight:** What a reader remembers is not merely an imperfect copy of the book. It is a second work that reveals their assumptions, priorities and identity.

A prototype could periodically ask for a retelling, compare it with the source and alter later emphasis without turning the experience into a quiz.

## Best wildcard ideas

### 1. Evidence-Harvest Book

**Non-obvious insight:** Some chapters should not be written by the author. They should be supplied by the reader’s world.

A book on cities might require observing five informal boundaries. A book on leadership might require interviewing someone who rejected authority. A novel might require finding a local object matching a fictional clue. Every copy would become partly local and unrepeatable.

### 2. Secret-Sharing Edition

**Non-obvious insight:** Reading does not have to be individually complete. Deliberately fragmented understanding can make dependence on other minds the core medium.

One reader might receive motives, another consequences, another chronology and another unreliable testimony. The full work exists only in conversation among them.
