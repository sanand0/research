## IDEATE

### 1. Five perspectives

The aim here is breadth, not feasibility. These produce 20 initial candidates.

**A patient with hourly work, transport uncertainty, and caring duties**

1. Replace exact appointment times with 60–90-minute arrival windows.
2. Allow one-tap cancellation without losing waiting-list priority.
3. Send a “leave now” prompt based on the patient’s chosen transport mode.
4. Batch appointments for members of the same household.

**A community health worker**

5. Have a local-language navigator personally confirm appointments.
6. Let patients nominate an “appointment buddy” who receives coordination prompts.
7. Offer a nearby community-clinic fallback when hospital attendance becomes difficult.
8. Pool transport for patients travelling from the same neighbourhood.

**A hospital operations chief**

9. Overbook clinics using predicted no-show probabilities.
10. Make bookings tentative until reconfirmed shortly before the visit.
11. Tell patients whether the clinic is running on time before they leave home.
12. Automatically offer released slots to wait-listed patients.

**A behavioural scientist**

13. Use social-norm messages about other patients attending.
14. Ask patients to state exactly how and when they will travel.
15. Require a tiny pre-visit action, such as answering two clinical questions.
16. Reward attendance with transport credits or service benefits.

**An airline network controller**

17. Use risk-tiered overbooking rather than a single overbooking rate.
18. Maintain an opt-in short-notice standby pool.
19. Give patients who release a slot early protected access to rebooking.
20. “Reaccommodate” patients to another location, time, or modality rather than treating attendance as binary.

### 2. Ideas imported from unrelated domains

#### Domain A: Fault-tolerant computing

**Atomic rule 1: Health checks detect impending failure before the service is needed.**

21. Run a 72-hour readiness check asking whether attendance is certain, uncertain, or impossible.
22. Detect missing prerequisites—tests, fasting, referrals, transport—and resolve them before appointment day.

**Atomic rule 2: Graceful degradation preserves the core outcome when the ideal service fails.**

23. Convert a missed full appointment into a lab-only or imaging-only visit, followed by remote review.
24. Offer an immediate telehealth fallback rather than rescheduling the entire care episode.

**Atomic rule 3: Redundancy activates when the primary path is at risk.**

25. Activate a nominated companion when the patient reports uncertainty.
26. Activate a backup patient when the original slot is released.

#### Domain B: Theme parks

**Atomic rule 1: Virtual queues separate waiting from physical presence.**

27. Let patients check in remotely before travelling.
28. Notify patients when the clinician is approaching readiness.

**Atomic rule 2: Entry windows absorb variability better than exact timestamps.**

29. Use arrival windows for clinics where examination duration is unpredictable.
30. Maintain rolling same-day appointment windows rather than fixed slots.

**Atomic rule 3: Demand is shaped by exposing capacity and making alternatives attractive.**

31. Show quieter clinic periods and offer targeted benefits for shifting.
32. Create a real-time slot-swap system between compatible patients.

This produces **32 raw candidates**.

### 3. Conventional ideas banned

The following are removed, along with close variants:

1. **Generic SMS, email, WhatsApp, or robocall reminders.** This includes merely changing wording, frequency, or channel.
2. **Fines, deposits, or punitive loss of access.** These risk worsening inequity and may hide rather than solve barriers.
3. **Blanket or algorithmic overbooking.** It transfers uncertainty into crowding and excessive waiting.
4. **Manual calls to every patient.** Difficult to scale and primarily treats symptoms.
5. **Generic attendance rewards or awareness campaigns.** These assume motivation is the main constraint.

A readiness interaction survives only when it changes the care pathway; it is not merely a reminder.

### 4. Merged, diverse survivor set

Overlapping candidates are consolidated into eight mechanisms.

#### 1. Readiness handshake

Seventy-two hours before the appointment, ask:

* “I will attend.”
* “I am unsure.”
* “I need another option.”

An uncertain response opens a short blocker menu: transport, work, caregiving, symptoms, preparation, cost, or other. Each blocker triggers an appropriate intervention.

This merges ideas 10, 14, 21 and 22.

#### 2. Care failover ladder

Treat the objective as **completing the next clinically useful step**, not necessarily attending the original appointment.

Possible fallbacks include:

* Original hospital visit.
* Nearby hospital or community clinic.
* Telehealth consultation.
* Lab or imaging visit followed by remote review.
* Pharmacy or nurse-led intervention.
* Rapid rebooking without restarting the referral process.

This merges ideas 7, 20, 23 and 24.

#### 3. Elastic arrival windows and virtual queuing

For suitable clinics, give patients an arrival window rather than an exact minute. Let them check in remotely and receive live guidance about when to travel.

This merges ideas 1, 11 and 27–30.

#### 4. Slot liquidity system

Make appointments easy to release and rapidly reusable:

* Releasing early preserves the patient’s waiting-list position.
* A standby cohort opts into short-notice offers.
* Compatible patients can accept or swap slots.
* Unclaimed offers automatically cascade to the next patient.

This merges ideas 2, 12, 18, 19, 26 and 32.

#### 5. Pre-visit micro-commitment

Ask the patient to complete a small clinically useful action: answer two questions, upload a medication photograph, select transport plans, or confirm preparation instructions.

Failure to complete it does not cancel care. It triggers assistance or an alternative pathway.

#### 6. Schedule the household, not just the patient

With consent, coordinate appointments around caregivers, companions, school schedules, and other household healthcare visits. A nominated buddy can help with transport, reminders and communication.

This merges ideas 4, 6 and 25.

#### 7. Reliability reciprocity contract

The hospital makes a commitment as well:

* Notify patients when the clinic is delayed.
* Publish expected waiting ranges.
* Offer remote check-in.
* Provide a rapid alternative when the hospital cancels or substantially delays an appointment.

The system treats no-shows partly as a trust and service-reliability problem, not purely as patient non-compliance.

#### 8. Neighbourhood access mesh

For geographic clusters with high non-attendance:

* Pool transport on clinic days.
* Route suitable care to community sites.
* Coordinate appointments from the same area.
* Use targeted transport support rather than generic rewards.

This merges ideas 3, 7, 8 and 31.

## Best practical ideas

### 1. Combine the readiness handshake with slot liquidity

This is the strongest practical intervention.

The non-obvious insight is that an “unsure” patient is not yet a no-show. It is a recoverable state. Today, most systems wait until the slot has been wasted. The handshake identifies uncertainty while there is still time either to remove the barrier or reclaim the capacity.

A simple flow:

1. Contact the patient 72 hours before the appointment.
2. Accept “yes,” “unsure,” or “need another option”; do not force a false confirmation.
3. Offer blocker-specific assistance or protected rescheduling.
4. Immediately release unwanted slots to an opt-in standby pool.
5. Preserve priority for patients who release their appointment.

The primary outcome should be **completed care episodes**, not merely attendance at the original appointment.

### 2. Introduce elastic arrival windows in selected clinics

Start with clinics where consultation length varies substantially and patients commonly wait despite arriving punctually.

The non-obvious insight is that a patient can be classified as a no-show for failing to meet an exact time even though the hospital itself operates with wide timing uncertainty. Sharing that uncertainty through windows and live queue information makes the arrangement more symmetric.

### 3. Add clinically useful micro-commitments

This is the fastest low-cost experiment. The task should prepare the visit rather than manipulate the patient—for example, confirming medication, symptoms or transport arrangements.

The non-obvious insight is that non-completion is valuable early-warning information. It should trigger help, not cancellation.

## Best wildcard ideas

### 1. Care failover ladder

Redefine the operating metric from:

> Did the patient attend this appointment?

to:

> Was the next useful step in the care pathway completed within an acceptable period?

A patient unable to attend a specialist consultation might still complete blood tests, imaging, medication review or a remote assessment. This could reduce both no-shows and the clinical harm caused by them.

### 2. Reliability reciprocity contract

Treat the appointment as a mutual commitment. The patient communicates uncertainty early; the hospital communicates delay and cancellation risk early.

The underlying insight is that repeated hospital delays train patients to view appointment times as approximate. A no-show intervention that demands patient punctuality without improving hospital reliability may have a low ceiling.

## Recommended pilot

Run the combined **readiness handshake plus slot-liquidity system** in several high-volume clinics, while retaining comparable clinics under the current process.

Measure:

* Original-slot no-show rate.
* Early-release rate.
* Percentage of released slots refilled.
* Completed care episodes within 7 and 30 days.
* Patient waiting time.
* Staff workload.
* Emergency visits following missed appointments.
* Results by age, language, disability, income proxy and digital access.

Essential guardrails are that uncertainty must never cause denial of care, no-show predictions must not reduce access, every digital path needs a telephone or in-person equivalent, and fallback modalities must be clinically approved.
