## 1. BAN: obvious ideas excluded

These are the ideas a smart generalist would probably produce in 30 seconds. Their core mechanisms—and thin disguises—are banned from everything below.

1. **More reminders:** SMS, WhatsApp, calls, email sequences, chatbots, personalized reminder wording, countdowns, or “smart nudges.”
2. **Financial consequences:** deposits, no-show fees, refundable bonds, points, lotteries, rewards, or loss-aversion framing.
3. **Overbooking:** booking extra patients, probabilistic overbooking, wait-room buffers, or airline-style capacity models.
4. **Easier cancellation or rescheduling:** portals, one-click links, conversational scheduling, self-service apps, or improved call centres.
5. **Transport assistance:** taxi credits, bus vouchers, hospital shuttles, parking subsidies, or route instructions.
6. **Patient education:** explaining the cost of missed appointments, emphasizing health consequences, commitment pledges, or guilt-based messaging.
7. **Risk prediction and targeted outreach:** no-show scores, demographic segmentation, machine-learning prioritization, or extra calls to “high-risk” patients.

The ideas below may involve communication or schedule changes operationally, but none depends on these banned mechanisms.

## 2.3 Borrow structures from unrelated domains

### Domain A: Software fault tolerance

Atomic structural rules:

1. **Graceful degradation:** when the preferred mode fails, deliver a reduced service rather than nothing.
2. **Checkpointing:** preserve completed work so that interruption does not force a full restart.
3. **Late binding:** delay irreversible resource assignment until better information is available.

Candidates that depend on these rules:

* **Auto-Downgrade Encounter** depends on graceful degradation. Without an alternate clinical mode, it is merely post-no-show outreach.
* **Split-Visit Checkpoint** and **Home Artifact Trigger** depend on checkpointing. The preliminary work must remain clinically useful even if the main visit fails.
* **Elastic Attendance Window** and **Departure-Triggered Dispatch** depend on late binding. Exact clinician capacity is assigned after the patient’s availability becomes less uncertain.

Rejected borrowing:

* **“Fault-tolerant reminder ladder”** was rejected because it is only reminders disguised as redundancy.
* **Redundant double-booking** was rejected because it is overbooking dressed as hedged computation.

### Domain B: Kidney-exchange networks

Atomic structural rules:

1. **Transfer converts incompatibility into matching:** a participant need not use the exact resource originally assigned.
2. **Chains unlock more value than isolated swaps:** A can help B, B can help C, and C can resolve A’s constraint.
3. **A neutral clearinghouse enables fair exchange:** participants need not reveal details or negotiate directly.

Candidates that depend on these rules:

* **Protected Slot Relay** depends on transferability: releasing a slot must not destroy the original patient’s future access.
* **Chain Swap Pool** depends on multi-way matching; otherwise it collapses into ordinary rescheduling.
* **Any-Clinic Claim Token** depends on neutral network clearing across hospital branches.

Rejected borrowing:

* **Appointment blockchain** was rejected. The distributed-ledger element adds decoration; an ordinary hospital-controlled clearing system provides the required mechanism.

# 3. CONVERGE

## 3.1 Falsification critique

### S1. Elastic Attendance Window

**Assumption that must hold:** A substantial share of no-shows results from uncertainty about work, caregiving, travel and hospital waiting—not refusal to seek care.

**Cheapest falsification:** Randomly offer 100 patients exact appointments and 100 patients guaranteed three-hour attendance windows with a remote queue. Compare completed visits and arrival clustering.

**Why obvious ideas miss it:** Conventional scheduling treats precision as convenience. For patients with volatile lives, precision transfers all timing risk to the patient.

### S2. Checkpointed Care

**Assumption that must hold:** Completing an initial clinical step creates momentum and leaves useful work behind even if the main visit fails.

**Cheapest falsification:** For one specialty, ask 50 patients to complete a brief local or home-based measurement before the visit. Compare subsequent attendance with similar patients who receive an atomic appointment.

**Why obvious ideas miss it:** Standard operations define the appointment as an indivisible unit: either the patient completes everything or nothing has happened.

### S3. Auto-Downgrade Encounter

**Assumption that must hold:** A meaningful fraction of absent patients remain reachable and can receive clinically valid reduced-mode care at the scheduled time.

**Cheapest falsification:** For the next 30 missed follow-ups, attempt immediate nurse or clinician contact and record pickup, clinical usefulness and need for rebooking.

**Why obvious ideas miss it:** They equate attendance with physical presence rather than completion of the clinical objective.

**Limitation:** It salvages no-shows more directly than it prevents them, so it is weaker against the literal metric.

### S4. Protected Appointment Exchange

**Assumption that must hold:** Patients conceal emerging conflicts because releasing a scarce appointment risks being pushed to the back of the queue.

**Cheapest falsification:** In one high-demand specialty, manually guarantee a replacement within seven days to anyone releasing a slot at least two hours before it. Offer released capacity to a small standby pool. Track release and fill rates.

**Why obvious ideas miss it:** Easier cancellation still asks the patient to surrender something valuable. The missing ingredient is protection of future access, not interface convenience.

### S5. Neighborhood Pop-Up Batch

**Assumption that must hold:** No-shows are geographically clustered enough to support periodic local delivery.

**Cheapest falsification:** Map six months of missed visits by postal sector, specialty and travel time. Kill the idea if eligible demand is too dispersed to fill a half-day mobile session.

**Why obvious ideas miss it:** They treat travel as an individual subsidy problem rather than a network-routing problem.

### S6. Commute-Synchronized Scheduling

**Assumption that must hold:** Transport failures are predictable by origin, route, time and day.

**Cheapest falsification:** Link appointment outcomes for one clinic to patients’ approximate origins and public-transport schedules. Check whether specific route-time combinations have materially different attendance.

**Why obvious ideas miss it:** They treat transport as a distance problem. Reliability and connection timing may matter more than kilometres.

### S7. Leave Clearance First

**Assumption that must hold:** Patients frequently book before they know whether an employer, school or caregiver arrangement will permit attendance.

**Cheapest falsification:** Interview 50 recent no-shows using a fixed causal questionnaire. Then test an official pre-appointment attendance letter in one clinic.

**Why obvious ideas miss it:** They assume the patient is the sole decision-maker. In practice, employers and family members may control the patient’s time.

### S8. Companion Coupling

**Assumption that must hold:** Shared travel and social accountability outweigh the added coordination burden.

**Cheapest falsification:** Offer 100 suitable patients the option of coordinating their visit with a caregiver’s hospital trip or compatible appointment. Measure uptake and completion.

**Why obvious ideas miss it:** Most interventions act on an isolated patient, although healthcare attendance is often a household logistics problem.

### S9. Any-Clinic Claim Token

**Assumption that must hold:** Simple appointments are sufficiently standardized across branches, and site mismatch contributes materially to absence.

**Cheapest falsification:** Allow a small group of routine follow-up patients to redeem their appointment at any of two nearby branches for four weeks.

**Why obvious ideas miss it:** Each hospital optimizes its own calendar, while patients experience the chain as one service.

### S10. Reverse Appointment

**Assumption that must hold:** Many scheduled physical visits exist mainly because in-person booking is the default, not because examination or equipment is essential.

**Cheapest falsification:** Clinically review 100 missed appointments and classify how many could have been resolved through record review, messaging, laboratory orders or a brief call.

**Why obvious ideas miss it:** They ask, “How do we make patients attend?” rather than, “Why was physical attendance required before care could begin?”

## 3.2 Pairwise knockout

The criterion is: **Which mechanism is most likely to materially reduce no-shows across this public hospital chain?**

### Opening comparisons

**S1 Elastic Attendance Window defeats S6 Commute-Synchronized Scheduling.**
Commute synchronization handles predictable transport patterns. Elastic windows absorb transport, work, caregiving and hospital-delay uncertainty simultaneously.

**S2 Checkpointed Care defeats S3 Auto-Downgrade Encounter.**
Checkpointing can change behavior before the failure occurs. Auto-downgrading mostly reduces the clinical waste after a no-show.

**S4 Protected Appointment Exchange defeats S8 Companion Coupling.**
Companion coupling applies only to patients with suitable companions and compatible trips. Protected exchange can operate across a much larger population.

**S9 Any-Clinic Claim Token defeats S5 Neighborhood Pop-Up Batch.**
Both change location. Network portability uses existing sites and staffing; pop-up clinics require sufficient geographic density and mobile operations.

**S10 Reverse Appointment defeats S7 Leave Clearance First.**
Leave clearance attacks one important cause. Reverse appointments can remove the need to obtain leave at all for a substantial subset of visits.

### Second round

**S4 Protected Appointment Exchange defeats S9 Any-Clinic Claim Token.**
Cross-site portability helps when the assigned branch is wrong. Protected exchange works for conflicts involving time, site, work, illness and caregiving, while also recovering the released capacity.

**S1 Elastic Attendance Window defeats S2 Checkpointed Care.**
Checkpointing requires clinically appropriate pre-work and new workflows for each specialty. Elastic windows can cover a broader range of services with a common operating model.

### Final comparisons

**S4 Protected Appointment Exchange defeats S10 Reverse Appointment for near-term practical impact.**
Reverse appointments require clinical redesign, governance and careful selection. Protected exchange can be tested manually within an existing specialty.

**S1 Elastic Attendance Window defeats S4 Protected Appointment Exchange overall.**
Exchange still depends on patients recognizing and reporting conflicts. Elastic windows directly remove the requirement that patients predict their circumstances days or weeks in advance.

Reverse Appointment remains the strongest wildcard because its potential effect is structurally larger despite lower near-term feasibility.

# 4. Recommendations

## Best practical idea: Elastic Attendance Windows

Replace exact appointments for selected services with a guaranteed attendance window—for example, 9:00 a.m. to noon. Patients enter a virtual queue when they are ready to depart or arrive. The hospital assigns the exact service sequence using current arrivals and clinical priority.

**Non-obvious insight:** Many no-shows may be rational responses to excessive timing risk. The hospital currently asks patients to predict work, childcare and transport conditions weeks in advance while retaining the right to make them wait after arrival.

**First cheap test:** Run a two-week randomized pilot in one high-volume follow-up clinic:

* 100 patients retain exact appointments.
* 100 receive a three-hour guaranteed window.
* No new reminders, incentives or transport support.
* Compare completed visits, lateness, staff utilization, average waiting and arrival congestion.

Kill the idea if attendance does not improve or if bunching creates unacceptable clinical overload.

## Second practical idea: Protected Appointment Exchange

Give patients a transferable appointment right rather than an all-or-nothing reservation. A patient who releases an unusable slot receives a protected replacement within a defined period. A central clearing process gives the released slot to a medically compatible standby patient.

This is not easier cancellation: the core intervention is that releasing capacity no longer threatens the patient’s access to care.

**Non-obvious insight:** Scarcity can increase no-shows. Patients may retain appointments they probably cannot use because surrendering them means returning to a long queue.

**First cheap test:** Operate a manual exchange for one oversubscribed specialty:

* Recruit 30–50 voluntary standby patients.
* Guarantee releasers a replacement within seven days.
* Measure advance releases, successful fills, eventual completion by releasers and additional staff effort.

Kill the idea if few patients release appointments even with protected replacement, or if released slots cannot be matched quickly.

## Best wildcard: Reverse Appointments

For selected follow-ups, the hospital begins the clinical encounter asynchronously. A clinician reviews the case, results and patient-submitted information first. The patient receives a physical appointment only when examination, equipment or complex discussion is necessary.

**Non-obvious insight:** A no-show is partly a property of service design. An appointment cannot be missed when care has already begun and physical presence is requested only after its necessity is established.

**First cheap test:** Review 100 recent missed appointments in two specialties. Have clinicians classify each as:

* Physical examination essential
* Could have begun asynchronously
* Could have been completed remotely
* Appointment probably unnecessary

Proceed to a 50-patient pilot only if at least roughly one-quarter could avoid or defer the initial physical visit.
