## Strategic thesis

The chain should stop behaving like a less convenient warehouse and become **the local operating system for reading**: a membership network where people discover books, develop reading habits, meet others, circulate books, create content, and receive expert guidance.

Physical bookselling itself is not inevitably declining. In an American Booksellers Association survey, 73.3% of respondents reported higher sales in 2025; Barnes & Noble opened its 50th new store of 2025 while crediting greater control by local booksellers; and Circana reports strong 2026 growth in tactile, screen-free categories and manga. The inference is that physical stores can grow, but primarily through curation, participation, locality, and physical experience—not merely inventory. ([the American Booksellers Association][1])

A suitable three-year definition of “thriving” would be:

* Positive same-store growth and double-digit store contribution margins.
* At least 30% of revenue associated with identified members.
* At least 25% of gross profit coming from subscriptions, services, institutional contracts, resale, publishing, or publisher services.
* Each store differentiated locally while sharing one chain-wide inventory and customer network.

# IDEATE

## 1. Five people who would see the problem differently

### A. Bookstore chief financial officer

1. Sell a monthly membership containing book credit, return privileges, and store access.
2. Turn slow stock into a chain-wide buyback and resale pool.
3. Operate curated libraries for employers, schools, hotels, and residential developments.
4. Charge publishers for controlled reader research and launch experiments—not merely shelf placement.

### B. Nightclub promoter

5. Replace occasional events with weekly rituals hosted by recognizable personalities.
6. Run three-month genre “seasons” ending in launches, performances, or overnight finales.
7. Match customers into small groups based on literary taste and social intent.
8. Create limited-capacity after-hours salons whose scarcity produces status.

### C. Multiplayer-game designer

9. Organize customers into reading guilds with cooperative missions and progression.
10. Create a chain-wide passport rewarding visits to differently themed stores.
11. Let neighbourhood reading achievements unlock donations, celebrations, or new stock.
12. Make selected books access keys to live experiences and hidden content.

### D. Librarian and urban planner

13. Sell access to quiet, screen-light communal space between a library and coworking facility.
14. Win municipal and employer contracts for literacy, ageing, wellbeing, and screen-free programmes.
15. Record and publish neighbourhood oral histories and local knowledge.
16. Offer personal “reading prescriptions” rather than leaving customers to browse alone.

### E. Marketplace and logistics entrepreneur

17. Promise access to anything held anywhere in the chain, with rapid inter-store transfer.
18. Create a resale marketplace whose proceeds return as store credit.
19. Provide print-on-demand and commercial infrastructure for local creators.
20. Let communities preorder and vote titles, genres, and programmes into existence.

## 2. Ideas transferred from two unrelated domains

### Domain A: Airlines

**Atomic structural rules**

1. Inventory belongs to the network, not an individual location.
2. Unused time-bound capacity expires and should be yield-managed.
3. Loyalty is strongest when it confers status and privileges, not only discounts.

**Derived candidates**

21. Treat every copy in every store as one virtual inventory pool, with a chain-wide availability promise.
22. Sell different store uses by daypart: quiet mornings, classrooms after school, private salons at night.
23. Create membership status that unlocks advance copies, reserved seating, first access, and concierge attention.

### Domain B: Public health

**Atomic structural rules**

1. Prevention is often more valuable than intervention after failure.
2. Trusted local nodes can deliver programmes better than distant central institutions.
3. Different populations require different protocols rather than one universal service.

**Derived candidates**

24. Sell reading-habit programmes for children, distracted adults, new parents, and seniors.
25. Become an outsourced literacy and screen-wellbeing provider for institutions.
26. Diagnose reading barriers and prescribe personalised, progressively challenging reading paths.

## 3. Ban the obvious ideas

These five conventional responses—and close variants—are excluded as core strategies:

1. **Add a café.**
2. **Run more ordinary author talks, book clubs, and children’s story hours.**
3. **Launch a points-based discount programme.**
4. **Improve the website, offer delivery, or attempt to price-match online retailers.**
5. **Allocate more space to gifts, stationery, toys, and generic merchandise.**

They may support a distinctive strategy, but none creates a defensible reason for the chain to exist.

# Recommendations

## Best practical idea 1: The membership flywheel

Combine:

* Reading Commons membership.
* Seasonal reading guilds.
* Books-as-access-keys.
* Human recommendation concierge.

A member receives monthly book credit, generous exchange privileges, reserved quiet-space access, a named bookseller, and entry to one seasonal guild. Higher tiers receive advance copies, small-group salons and priority access.

The programme should not lead with savings. Its promise should be:

> “Read more, choose better, and find your people.”

**Non-obvious insight:** people are unlikely to subscribe to a bookstore merely for cheaper books. They may subscribe to a persistent identity, ritual, place and relationship that improves their life.

Start with three or four tightly defined guilds—such as manga, speculative fiction, ambitious nonfiction and parents reading with children—rather than dozens of generic clubs.

## Best practical idea 2: Circular certainty

Combine:

* A 30-day taste guarantee.
* Guaranteed buyback values for selected books.
* Store-credit wallets.
* Chain-wide used inventory.
* Reliable transfer of titles between locations.

For example, a customer buying a curated selection could return it within 30 days for most of its value in store credit, or sell it back later at a published declining value. Returned books feed a used-book marketplace, starter bundles, school libraries and guild reading packs.

**Non-obvious insight:** the principal barrier to physical discovery is not always price; it is the risk of buying the wrong object. Online retail optimises known-item purchasing. The chain can own low-risk exploration.

This also creates a repeat-visit loop:

**discover → buy → read → return or resell → receive credit → discover again.**

## Best practical idea 3: B2B Reading-as-a-Service

Sell annual contracts to:

* Employers seeking learning and screen-wellbeing programmes.
* Schools needing book fairs, class sets and teacher curation.
* Condominiums, hotels and hospitals needing managed libraries.
* Senior communities seeking facilitated reading and oral-history programmes.

A contract can include rotating collections, employee credits, hosted cohorts, family programmes, usage reporting and local-store access.

**Non-obvious insight:** the chain’s highest-value customer may be an organisation paying to create behaviour—learning, connection, literacy or wellbeing—rather than an individual buying a book.

This supplies recurring revenue while using staff and space during otherwise weak periods.

## Best wildcard idea 1: The publisher demand laboratory

Turn the chain into a geographically distributed test market.

Publishers could pay to:

* Test covers, positioning and formats with recruited reader panels.
* Run controlled prerelease cohorts.
* Measure browsing, sampling and conversion.
* Identify overlooked backlist titles.
* Test local demand before committing inventory.
* Find emerging themes through anonymised recommendation and preorder data.

Community-funded shelves can supply genuine demand signals. Successful local creators can graduate into the chain’s micro-press, giving the retailer selective ownership of intellectual property.

**Non-obvious insight:** online platforms know what customers click. A bookstore chain can learn what people touch, discuss, abandon, recommend, finish and lend—richer signals for certain publishing decisions.

Customer consent and an explicit prohibition on selling personally identifiable reading histories would be essential.

## Best wildcard idea 2: Turn the chain into a genre district

Standardised stores should become deliberately different.

One location might be the city’s destination for manga and Japanese culture; another for children’s discovery; another for speculative worlds and games; another for ideas, history and public debate. Each retains a useful general range but possesses one destination-level speciality.

A chain passport, transfer service and seasonal narrative link the locations. Customers travel across the network to complete collections, attend finales or reach membership status.

**Non-obvious insight:** having multiple stores is currently a duplication cost. Specialisation can convert it into a network effect: each store advertises and completes the others.

# Three-year execution

## Months 0–6: Prove behaviour

Select six representative stores, paired with control stores.

Launch:

* A simple paid membership with book credit and exchange privileges.
* Four seasonal guilds led by the chain’s strongest booksellers.
* A controlled buyback programme covering 200–300 titles.
* A small B2B sales team pursuing ten anchor contracts.
* Named bookseller profiles and customer preference memory.

Measure cohort renewal, visits per member, book-credit redemption, buyback economics, inventory turns, member gross margin and B2B acquisition cost.

## Months 7–18: Build the network

Roll successful membership elements across the chain. Introduce the unified inventory layer and inter-store transfers. Give local managers explicit assortment authority within financial constraints—consistent with the local-control strategy associated with recent Barnes & Noble expansion. ([barnesandnobleinc.com][2])

Specialise selected stores around demonstrated local demand. Train booksellers as hosts and concierges, with compensation partly tied to membership retention rather than only transactions.

Pilot the publisher laboratory with two cooperative publishers and strict privacy rules.

## Months 19–36: Reshape the portfolio

Classify stores into four roles:

1. **Destination stores:** deep genre specialisation.
2. **Neighbourhood commons:** membership, guilds and family programmes.
3. **Institutional hubs:** fulfilment and B2B programme delivery.
4. **Compact satellites:** curated retail, pickup and membership acquisition.

Relocate, shrink or close stores that remain undifferentiated commodity outlets. Expand the micro-press selectively, based on demonstrated preorder and guild demand rather than editorial intuition alone.

By the end of year three, the chain should no longer describe itself primarily as a seller of books. It should be a **membership, circulation, community and publishing network whose most important physical interface happens to be the bookstore**.
