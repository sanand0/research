## FRAME

**Binding constraint:** Appointments are allocated as rigid, exclusive promises weeks ahead, while patients’ ability and need to attend remain volatile; when circumstances change, the care unit cannot switch time, site, channel, actor, or scope without becoming a no-show.

Use **clinically useful encounters completed within the intended care window** as the primary outcome. Raw administrative no-show rate is secondary because merely deleting appointments or relabelling them as telephone visits can game it.

**The 30-second mode—banned, including thin disguises:**

1. More SMS, calls, WhatsApp reminders, nudges, or cleverer reminder wording.
2. Fines, deposits, attendance rewards, loyalty points, or commitment contracts.
3. Transport, childcare, parking, or similar barrier-removal vouchers.
4. AI no-show prediction followed by targeted reminders or navigators.
5. Blanket, dynamic, or risk-adjusted overbooking.
6. Easier cancellation/rescheduling followed by a conventional standby list.
7. Patient education, counselling, or accountability calls about the harm of missing visits.

No candidate below uses these core mechanisms.

## DIVERGE

### Independent dimensions

1. **Reserved unit:** exact slot, broad window, care episode, or clinical milestone.
2. **What may change:** time, site, clinician, modality, attendee, or visit scope.
3. **Uncertainty owner:** patient, household, employer, clinic team, or hospital chain.
4. **Failure response:** substitute, decompose, relocate, defer conditionally, or eliminate.
5. **Care locus:** hospital, community anchor, workplace, home, or mobile route.
6. **Timing:** at discharge, weeks before, same day, after lateness, or only when disease state changes.

### Borrowed domain 1: cloud computing

Atomic rules:

1. Bind work to any compatible resource only when execution begins.
2. Fail over automatically when the preferred channel fails.
3. Pool variable demand rather than reserving one machine per future job.

Candidates dependent on these rules: chain-wide care windows, modality failover, and rolling-entry clinics.

### Borrowed domain 2: condition-based industrial maintenance

Atomic rules:

1. Trigger service from observed condition, not merely elapsed calendar time.
2. Bundle maintenance with an already unavoidable shutdown or visit.
3. Decompose work into independently completable modules.

Candidates dependent on these rules: triggered follow-up, anchor-event care, and modular visits.

### Candidate pool after merging causal overlaps

1. **Chain-wide care window:** reserve a two-hour specialty window redeemable at any compatible hospital rather than one site and minute. **Typicality 0.24**
2. **Automatic modality failover:** an unarrived eligible patient’s visit switches at appointment time to telephone, video, or asynchronous review without rebooking. **0.28**
3. **Condition-triggered follow-up:** stable chronic patients receive appointments only when home measurements, tests, symptoms, or patient request cross a threshold. **0.16**
4. **Anchor-event clinic:** attach specialist care to an event the patient already reliably attends, such as dialysis, laboratory testing, medication collection, or another clinic. **0.18**
5. **Modular encounter:** separate history, measurements, tests, decisions, and counselling so any completed module advances the care episode. **0.21**
6. **Certainty-class booking:** patients choose either an exact-time reservation or a broader service window based on how predictable their lives currently are. **0.34**
7. **Venue-preserving switch:** retain the appointment time while allowing arrival at another hospital, community booth, or assisted telehealth room. **0.23**
8. **Authorized proxy encounter:** a nominated caregiver may attend for history, results, care planning, and instructions when the patient cannot. **0.31**
9. **Employer protected-time handshake:** booking creates a privacy-preserving work-release credential accepted by participating employers. **0.19**
10. **Rolling-entry group clinic:** compatible patients enter a continuous education-and-assessment session with private clinician breakouts rather than individual start times. **0.37**
11. **Mobile care route:** a multidisciplinary team follows recurring geographic routes, and patients attend the nearest temporary node. **0.29**
12. **Late-arrival guarantee:** arrival anytime inside a published tolerance band guarantees same-day completion through pooled clinicians. **0.32**
13. **Hospital reliability contract:** the hospital guarantees a bounded start delay, making its side of the appointment credible before seeking stronger patient commitment. **0.11**
14. **Household-substitutable capacity:** where clinically appropriate, an unused primary-care slot may be transferred to an eligible household member. **0.07**
15. **Dual-use clinical session:** clinicians maintain useful asynchronous work that automatically occupies unused encounter time, challenging whether no-show reduction is the real bottleneck. **0.26**
16. **Specialist delegation package:** routine review moves to a community clinician with structured specialist e-consult escalation rather than requiring hospital attendance. **0.41**

Thirteen of the sixteen candidates score below 0.3.

## CONVERGE

### Causal survivors

1. **Chain-wide care window:** loosen time/site specificity → more feasible attendance opportunities → fewer abandoned care episodes; assumption: pooled variability exceeds arrival bunching; adopter: patients gain choice and hospitals gain cross-site utilisation; falsifier: completion stays flat while waits increase.
2. **Automatic modality failover:** travel or work disruption no longer destroys the encounter → clinician uses the reserved time through another channel → completed-care rate rises; assumption: a material share of visits is remotely useful; adopter: patients avoid rebooking and clinicians avoid idle time; falsifier: low conversion or high repeat-visit and safety burden.
3. **Condition-triggered follow-up:** remove routine appointments lacking current clinical need → remaining encounters carry stronger need and intent → fewer no-shows and more capacity; assumption: thresholds safely detect deterioration; adopter: patients avoid unnecessary trips and clinicians manage smaller purposeful panels; falsifier: delayed escalation or worse clinical outcomes.
4. **Anchor-event clinic:** eliminate a separate journey → fewer logistical failure points → attendance rises; assumption: enough patients have reliable anchor events; adopter: patients save a trip and departments share infrastructure; falsifier: low overlap or excessive clinician travel and setup time.
5. **Modular encounter:** allow partial clinical progress despite disrupted attendance → fewer episodes reset to zero → subsequent completion becomes easier; assumption: modules retain independent value; adopter: staff avoid repeated intake and patients preserve progress; falsifier: partial encounters rarely reach completed care.
6. **Authorized proxy encounter:** substitute an informed caregiver when the patient is unavailable → planning and result review still occur → fewer zero-value slots; assumption: proxy participation is clinically and legally sufficient for selected visits; adopter: families avoid rebooking; falsifier: low consent, low usage, or frequent duplicated visits.
7. **Employer protected-time handshake:** make work absence predictable and legitimate → reduce job-related conflicts → attendance rises; assumption: employers will recognise the credential; adopter: workers gain protection and employers gain scheduled rather than sudden absence; falsifier: employer uptake is low or work-conflict no-shows do not change.
8. **Hospital reliability contract:** reduce uncertainty about hospital delays → patients stop treating appointment times as approximate → commitment strengthens; assumption: expected hospital lateness materially causes disengagement; adopter: patients gain predictability and clinics gain smoother arrivals; falsifier: punctuality improves without changing attendance.

### Pairwise knockout

**Quarterfinals**

* Automatic modality failover defeats authorized proxy: it covers more patients with less consent and governance complexity.
* Anchor-event clinic defeats employer handshake: it removes the entire additional trip rather than only one source of scheduling conflict.
* Condition-triggered follow-up defeats modular encounters: eliminating unnecessary appointments is more consequential than salvaging parts of them.
* Chain-wide care windows defeat hospital reliability contracts: flexibility addresses more forms of uncertainty than punctuality alone.

**Semifinals**

* Automatic modality failover defeats anchor-event clinics: it can be deployed across more specialties without relocating staff.
* Condition-triggered follow-up defeats chain-wide care windows: where safe, removing calendar-based demand produces a larger structural gain than making it flexible.

**Final**

Automatic modality failover defeats condition-triggered follow-up on likelihood of surviving reality: it retains clinician review, requires less pathway redesign, and can be restricted visit by visit. Condition-triggered follow-up remains the higher-upside wildcard.

## RECOMMENDATIONS

### Best practical idea: automatic modality failover

**Design:** At booking, clinicians mark whether the visit is eligible for telephone, video, asynchronous review, or in-person only. If the patient has not arrived five minutes after the scheduled time, staff activate the safest eligible fallback. This is not a pre-appointment reminder and does not require the patient to reschedule.

**Non-obvious insight:** A no-show is often a failure of the chosen transport channel—physical presence—not a disappearance of the patient’s need or willingness to receive care. Preserve the scarce resource, the clinician’s reserved attention, while changing its delivery channel.

A retrospective cohort covering more than three million appointments found lower adjusted no-show odds for telephone and video encounters than for in-person visits, with telephone access particularly relevant to patients facing digital-access disparities. This supports channel flexibility, although it does not by itself prove that real-time failover will have the same effect. ([PubMed][1])

**First cheap test:** In two high-no-show clinics, mark eligible visits for eight weeks. For randomly selected clinic-days, make one outbound call after five minutes of non-arrival and complete the encounter by the safest allowed channel. Compare against control days on clinically completed encounters within 24 hours, repeat appointments within 30 days, clinician minutes, unresolved cases, and results by age, language, income proxy, and disability. Kill it if fewer than roughly one in five non-arrivals becomes a clinically useful completed encounter or if rework cancels the capacity gain.

### Second practical idea: anchor-event clinics

**Design:** Find events patients already attend reliably—dialysis, infusion, laboratory monitoring, medication collection, wound care, rehabilitation, or another specialty visit. Place a rotating clinician, nurse practitioner, or assisted telehealth booth at that event and complete compatible follow-up there.

**Non-obvious insight:** Reliability is often attached to an activity, not to a person. A patient who repeatedly misses cardiology may reliably attend dialysis three times weekly. Rather than trying to repair the unreliable journey, attach care to the reliable journey.

**First cheap test:** Mine six months of scheduling data to find patients who missed one specialty but attended another hospital service within seven days. Select the strongest recurring overlap, offer 100 eligible patients bundled care on their next anchor visit, and measure incremental completion, patient time, clinician travel/setup minutes, and duplicated work. Kill it if fewer than 30 eligible patients per month can be served per anchor location or if staffing cost exceeds the recovered clinical capacity.

### Best wildcard: condition-triggered follow-up

**Design:** For carefully selected stable chronic-care cohorts, replace fixed three- or six-month reviews with home measurements, periodic laboratory data, symptom questions, and guaranteed rapid access. Generate an appointment only when a threshold is crossed or the patient requests one.

**Non-obvious insight:** Some no-shows may be accurate patient signals that the calendar—not the patient—is wrong. When a stable patient sees little current value in a routine visit, stronger persuasion treats the symptom. Eliminate appointments whose timing is determined by convention rather than clinical state.

In a randomized trial involving stable rheumatoid-arthritis patients, self-monitoring with patient-initiated care produced fewer outpatient visits with similar measured disease outcomes to usual preplanned care, although the trial was small and condition-specific. ([PubMed][2])

**First cheap test:** Select 100 clinically stable patients from one chronic pathway with explicit clinician-approved escalation thresholds. Replace one routine follow-up with monitoring plus guaranteed review within a defined period when triggered. Compare clinical control, urgent utilisation, patient anxiety, staff workload, number of scheduled encounters, and completed-care capacity. Kill it immediately if threshold breaches are missed, escalation is delayed, or clinical outcomes worsen.

### Second wildcard: chain-wide care windows

**Design:** Allocate a small fraction of capacity as a shared specialty pool. A patient receives, for example, “Tuesday 1–3 p.m., any of these three hospitals,” with live guidance showing the least-loaded site. The patient owns access to capacity, not a particular chair.

**Non-obvious insight:** Exact appointments force the hospital to predict both patient circumstances and local capacity far in advance. Pooling lets independent variations cancel: one site’s excess arrivals can be balanced by another site’s spare capacity, and patients can adapt after their day becomes clearer.

**First cheap test:** Dedicate one half-day per week in a high-volume, low-procedure specialty across two nearby hospitals. Give 150 patients a common 90-minute window and either-site access, with strict arrival caps and a conventional-booking control group. Measure care completion, travel distance, waiting-time distribution, staff overtime, cross-site switching, and patient preference. Kill it if arrivals cluster enough to create materially longer waits without a corresponding increase in completion.
