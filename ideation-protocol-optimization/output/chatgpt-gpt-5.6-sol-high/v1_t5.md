## IDEATE

### 1. Five perspectives

These 15 candidates are intentionally unfiltered and may overlap.

**Traffic-network engineer**

1. Add lanes or reversible peak-direction lanes.
2. Deploy citywide adaptive signal coordination.
3. Meter vehicles at garage and neighborhood exits, releasing them in short pulses.

**Behavioral economist**
4. Introduce congestion tolls and dynamic parking prices.
5. Run real-time “trip demand response”: reward people for cancelling or delaying trips during imminent overload.
6. Offer an emergency-mobility guarantee to people who leave their cars at home.

**Last-mile logistics dispatcher**
7. Create a common exchange for spare capacity on taxis, shuttles, school buses and municipal fleets.
8. Route parcels through neighborhood microhubs for consolidated final delivery.
9. Operate a neighborhood errand utility that bundles groceries, returns, prescriptions and school pickups.

**Community organizer**
10. Expand rail and bus capacity.
11. Bring pop-up clinics, government services, markets and libraries into high-trip neighborhoods.
12. Let neighborhoods create time-based street-access compacts for residents, deliveries, play and through-traffic.

**Commercial real-estate operator**
13. Promote remote work, flexible hours and conventional carpooling.
14. Create an office-swap marketplace: employees use another participating company’s workspace closer to home.
15. Give large destinations an hourly vehicle-arrival budget rather than regulating individual drivers.

### 2. Transfer from unrelated domains

#### Domain A: Computer networks

Atomic structural rules:

1. **Admission control:** refuse or delay new traffic before the network saturates.
2. **Backpressure:** tell sources to slow down when downstream capacity is exhausted.
3. **Multiplexing:** combine many small flows that share part of a route.

Derived candidates:

16. Place visitors in virtual queues at home when a destination’s surrounding roads approach saturation.
17. Apply backpressure to parking garages and residential exits rather than letting queues spill onto arterial roads.
18. Require booking, retail and government-service apps to expose a “complete without travelling” alternative.
19. Create a common dispatch protocol that assigns unused fleet capacity across operators.
20. During overload, switch the road network into a protected mode with a small number of guaranteed-flow corridors.

#### Domain B: Forest-fire ecology

Atomic structural rules:

1. **Firebreaks:** selectively disconnect a network to prevent a cascading spread.
2. **Fuel reduction:** removing a small amount near a critical threshold can prevent a large disaster.
3. **Distributed refuges:** providing resources locally reduces dangerous mass movement.

Derived candidates:

21. Dynamically close a mathematically selected set of road links to stop spillback propagating across districts.
22. Trigger small, targeted trip-curtailment requests only when traffic is near its collapse threshold.
23. Establish temporary “service refuges” where civic and commercial services move into affected neighborhoods.
24. Require new developments to place trip capacity in escrow by funding verified trip reductions elsewhere.
25. Divide the city into temporary local-access cells during severe congestion, preventing unnecessary cross-city movement.

### 3. Conventional ideas banned

The five most obvious approaches, including near-duplicates, are removed:

1. **Build more roads:** new lanes, flyovers, tunnels and reversible-lane variants.
2. **Expand conventional public transit:** more buses, rail lines or frequency as the primary intervention.
3. **Charge drivers more:** congestion tolls, cordon pricing and dynamic parking prices.
4. **Optimize traffic signals:** adaptive signals, green waves and turn restrictions.
5. **Ask commuters to reorganize voluntarily:** remote work, flexible hours and conventional carpool campaigns.

### 4. Merged surviving set

Overlapping concepts were combined: network backpressure became origin pulse metering; fleet exchanges were merged; service refuges became mobile service districts; and street compacts, protected corridors and cut-set closures became adaptive traffic firebreaks.

The result is 12 mechanisms spanning network control, behavior, logistics, service delivery, workplaces, regulation and land use.

## Recommendations

### Best practical ideas

**1. Trip demand response with an emergency-mobility guarantee**

The underlying insight is that congestion is often a threshold problem, not a linear one. The city may not need to reduce peak traffic by 20%; a precisely timed reduction of a few percent could prevent network collapse. The emergency guarantee makes participation safer for households that keep a car available “just in case.”

A pilot could recruit volunteers in several congested corridors and issue requests only during predicted breakdown conditions.

**2. Destination virtual queues**

The road queue is partly an information failure: thousands of people leave simultaneously because nobody is told that the destination or its access roads are temporarily saturated. Hospitals, government offices, schools, malls and venues could delay admission slots, preserve a person’s place and offer benefits for waiting elsewhere.

The non-obvious move is to relocate waiting time rather than merely shorten it.

**3. Origin pulse metering**

Instead of allowing every garage and side street to inject vehicles continuously, the city would briefly hold and release vehicles in coordinated batches. This is most practical around stadium garages, business districts, ports and large residential developments.

The key insight is that a visible two-minute wait before entering the network may prevent an invisible twenty-minute delay after entering it.

### Best wildcard ideas

**4. Adaptive traffic firebreaks**

Most traffic systems try to maximize connectivity. The wildcard proposition is that, during impending gridlock, selected temporary disconnections may protect the wider network. A small changing set of streets would become local-access-only, preventing blocked intersections from propagating congestion across districts.

The non-obvious insight is that connectivity is beneficial under normal load but can transmit failure under extreme load.

**5. Office-swap marketplace**

Participating employers would make spare desks available across the city. An employee could work from another company’s secure facility near home while remaining connected to their actual team.

The idea separates two things normally treated as inseparable: an employer and the physical location from which its employee works. It could reduce commute distance without requiring permanent remote work, residential relocation or new transport infrastructure.
