# FRAME

**Binding constraint:** DAU isn't limited by acquisition or motivation-to-improve — it's limited by the fact that "open the app" requires the user to first notice a *specific moment* where meditation is the right tool, and that noticing almost never happens outside the 5-10 min the user already dedicates to "wellness time." The app is invisible during the other 23.5 hours where the actual triggering emotional events occur.

**The mode (banned, plus thin disguises):**
1. Smarter/personalized push notifications (time-of-day ML, adaptive reminders)
2. Streaks/badges/leaderboards/social gamification
3. Bite-sized micro-sessions (1-min, "just breathe" quick actions)
4. Habit-stacking prompts ("meditate after brushing teeth")
5. Content variety / celebrity or new instructor drops
6. Social/community features (friends, groups, accountability partners)
7. AI chatbot companion for check-ins

Banned mechanism = "prompt or content delivered *to* the user, hoping to catch them in a receptive moment they define themselves." Any idea that's still "we send something, user opens app" is a disguise of #1/#7 and is out.

# DIVERGE

**Independent dimensions:** (a) trigger source — who/what decides *when* is the right moment; (b) locus of value — does value happen inside or outside the app; (c) actor — solo user vs. others (family, coworkers, strangers); (d) payer — who funds engagement (user, employer, insurer, third party); (e) time horizon — instant moment vs. seasonal/life-event; (f) what's removed — subtract a feature/friction rather than add one.

Candidates across combos:

1. Detect via phone sensors (typing speed, call just ended, calendar event marked "stressful") that a stress-spike moment occurred, and open a silent micro-offer only then — trigger source = device signal, not user. (typicality 0.25)
2. Partner with calendar/email apps so a "hard meeting" auto-inserts a 90-second pre-meeting session as a calendar block, not a notification — locus = inside another app's workflow. (0.2)
3. Let a *third party* (spouse, manager, therapist) send a one-tap "thinking of you, take 3 minutes" nudge to the user — actor = someone else initiates. (0.15)
4. Employer pays per-employee for "meditation minutes redeemable as PTO minutes" — payer = employer, value = tangible currency outside app. (0.1)
5. Remove the session entirely: app's core loop becomes logging *urges to meditate that you didn't act on*, and only afterward suggests the shortest viable practice — subtract completion pressure. (0.15)
6. Seasonal/life-event targeting: app auto-activates a distinct mode around known population-wide stress windows (tax day, back-to-school, exam weeks) via public data, not personal data — time horizon = calendar-macro not daily-micro. (0.2)
7. Ambient hardware trigger: a physical object (doorknob sticker, car dash clip) that's the actual cue, app is secondary interface — mechanism = environment redesign. (0.15)

**Borrowing from 2 unrelated domains:**

*Domain A — Restaurant kitchens (mise en place):* rule 1: prep happens before demand arrives; rule 2: stations are physically arranged so no motion is wasted mid-task; rule 3: the pass rejects anything not ready, forcing upstream discipline.
→ Candidate: "Pre-loaded state" — app pre-selects and pre-loads (not just recommends) the exact session for a known recurring slot in the user's calendar the night before, so at the trigger moment there is zero decision, only a single tap — borrows rule 1+2 (prep before demand, zero wasted motion). (0.2)

*Domain B — Air traffic control:* rule 1: controllers manage by exception — silence is the default state, alerts only on deviation; rule 2: handoffs between controllers are explicit and logged, never ambiguous; rule 3: separation minimums are enforced structurally, not by reminding pilots to be careful.
→ Candidate: "Management by exception" — app goes silent by default and only surfaces when a *structural deviation* from the user's own baseline is detected (e.g., sleep app shows 3 nights of bad sleep, health data shows resting HR trending up) — it borrows "alert only on deviation from a defined baseline," not "alert on schedule." (0.25)
→ Candidate: "Explicit handoff" — when a user's therapist/coach session ends, that session app explicitly and automatically "hands off" the user to a meditation app with context pre-loaded (what was discussed), mimicking ATC handoff protocol — no independent re-triggering needed. (0.1)

**Typicality check:** pool of 10; below 0.3 = items 1,2,3,4,5,7, borrowed-A, borrowed-B1, borrowed-B2 (9 of 10) — well over half. Good spread.

**Merge by mechanism:**
- Sensor/deviation-triggered (1, borrowed-B1) → merge into one "baseline-deviation trigger" candidate.
- Third-party/relational initiation (3, borrowed-B2) → merge into "someone else triggers on your behalf."
- Calendar/workflow embedding (2, borrowed-A) → merge into "pre-loaded calendar embedding."
- Employer/macro-population (4, 6) → merge into "institutional/population-level activation."
- Environment/object cue (7) → stands alone.
- Friction-removal logging (5) → stands alone.

Final pool: 6 mechanism-distinct candidates, ~4x final target of 1-2 recommendations.

# CONVERGE

For each survivor — causal path, load-bearing assumption, adopter, falsifier:

**A. Baseline-deviation trigger** (phone/health-data signals user's state has structurally shifted, e.g., sleep or HR trending off-baseline) → causes the *only* alerts a user gets to feel earned/relevant instead of generic → raises open-rate per notification and reduces notification fatigue-driven uninstalls → moves DAU by keeping the small alert channel effective longer.
- Assumption: passive signals (sleep, HR, calendar density) actually correlate with "receptive moment for meditation" better than time-of-day guessing.
- Adopter: users who've already granted health-data permissions (health-conscious segment); they keep using it because alerts feel accurate, not spammy.
- Falsifier: A/B test — deviation-triggered alerts vs. best-in-class ML time-of-day alerts; if open rate isn't meaningfully higher, kill it. Cheap, fast.

**B. Someone-else-triggers** (a trusted person sends a nudge) → causes the trigger to arrive with social proof/warmth attached, which is a different psychological channel than app-generated prompts → increases completion rate of the specific session triggered → over time builds a habit loop anchored to relationships, which are stickier than app mechanics.
- Assumption: users have at least one person in their life willing to send these regularly (not just once as a gimmick).
- Adopter: couples/close friends who already do wellness-adjacent things together; sustained because reciprocity keeps both sides engaged.
- Falsifier: ship a "send a moment" button to 500 existing users, measure whether senders repeat within 2 weeks. If it's a one-and-done novelty, kill it.

**C. Pre-loaded calendar embedding** (session auto-prepped and inserted the night before into known recurring high-stress calendar slots) → causes decision cost at trigger moment to drop to ~zero → increases conversion from "moment arrives" to "session completed" → moves DAU because the biggest drop-off (moment→action) shrinks.
- Assumption: users' calendars reliably contain identifiable recurring stress events (not everyone calendars their stress).
- Adopter: knowledge workers with dense, labeled calendars (already a meditation-app-buying demographic).
- Falsifier: pull a sample of connected calendars, check % with regularly recurring, identifiable stress-pattern events. If <20%, mechanism has no surface area — kill it.

**D. Institutional/population-level activation** (employer-funded, or macro-event-triggered modes) → causes acquisition and reactivation to happen via a channel outside the app's own gravity (HR comms, tax-season news) → increases periodic reactivation spikes → moves DAU in bursts correlated to real-world stress calendar, not app mechanics.
- Assumption: employers/institutions will actually promote it repeatedly, not just at launch.
- Adopter: HR/benefits teams, renewed because it shows up positively in engagement metrics they report upward.
- Falsifier: check historical redemption-decay curves of any existing employer wellness perk; if usage always craters after month 1 regardless of content, this mechanism is not the fix — kill it.

**E. Environment/object cue** (physical object is real trigger, app is secondary) → causes trigger to exist in physical space where phone notifications can't (car, doorknob, desk) → increases moments where user considers meditating who never would've been reached digitally → moves DAU by expanding total addressable trigger-moments, not fighting for share of existing ones.
- Assumption: users will actually install/place a physical object and keep it in place over time (physical habit infrastructure is a big ask).
- Adopter: highly is intentional early-adopter users doing a deliberate ritual reset (e.g., car commuters, new parents).
- Falsifier: cheap mail-out of a sticker/clip to 200 users, track 30-day retention vs. control. If placement/attrition is high, kill it.

**F. Friction-removal / urge-logging** (log the *urge*, not the session; app's job is capturing near-misses) → causes the app to have data and presence at moments of intent even when no session happens → increases raw open frequency (logging is faster than a session) → moves DAU directly, independent of whether meditation itself increases.
- Assumption: users are willing to open an app to log a "non-action" — that this feels valuable rather than like admitting failure.
- Adopter: quantified-self-leaning users who already log other behaviors.
- Falsifier: prototype a single "I almost meditated" button, see if usage sustains past week 2 or dies as a novelty.

**Pairwise knockout (most consequential-if-works AND most likely to survive contact):**

- C vs A: A depends on noisy passive-sensor correlation (harder to get right, privacy-sensitive); C depends on data users already explicitly created (calendar labels) — C survives contact better, and its effect (killing the moment→action drop-off) is typically the single biggest leak in these funnels. **C beats A.**
- C vs E: E has bigger addressable-moment upside but very high behavioral cost (physical object adoption) and slow, uncertain rollout. C is faster to test and the effect size on an already-identified funnel leak is more certain. **C beats E** on likely-to-survive, though E remains the highest-consequence-if-it-works wildcard.
- B vs C: B's mechanism (relational trigger) is consequential and taps a totally different retention channel (stickier than app mechanics) but requires a two-sided behavior (someone else must act) — much higher structural fragility. C requires only one side to act. **C beats B** on survivability; B is the strongest wildcard for a fundamentally different retention shape.
- D vs C: D's effect is real but historically decays fast (institutional perks always do) — falsifier evidence is almost certainly against it. **C beats D.**
- F vs C: F increases raw opens but on a lower-value action (logging, not meditating) — less consequential if it works. **C beats F**, but F is the cheapest test in the whole pool.

**C wins the practical slot outright.**

For wildcard, compare the two strongest non-C survivors head-to-head: B (relational trigger) vs E (environment/object cue) — B has faster testability and a genuinely different retention mechanism (social/relational rather than any digital or physical redesign), which is more consequential if it works because it's uncorrelated with every other lever competitors already pull. **B wins the wildcard slot.**

---

## Recommendation

**Practical: Pre-loaded calendar embedding (C).** Non-obvious insight: the DAU leak isn't "user forgets to meditate," it's "user has 3 seconds of decision fatigue exactly when they're least equipped to make a decision" — so win by moving all the deciding to the night before, leaving only a tap. First cheap test: audit a sample of connected calendars for % with recurring, identifiable stress-pattern events (recurring 1:1s, "board prep," exam blocks) — if that surface area doesn't exist, the whole mechanism has no floor to stand on.

**Wildcard: Someone-else-triggers (B).** Non-obvious insight: competitors are all optimizing the *app→user* channel; the untapped channel is *human→human*, which carries social proof no push notification can fake and doesn't compete with notification fatigue at all. First cheap test: ship a single "send someone a moment" button to a few hundred existing users and check not send-rate, but *repeat*-send-rate within two weeks — that's the only number that tells you whether this becomes a habit loop or a one-time gimmick.
