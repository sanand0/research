# BAN: The Mode (5–7 obvious ideas)

A typical AI assistant, asked "reduce no-shows at a hospital," produces some subset of:

1. **SMS/text reminders** (24–48h before appointment)
2. **Automated phone call reminders / IVR**
3. **Overbooking** to statistically absorb expected no-shows
4. **Reminder + confirmation ("reply Y to confirm")** loop
5. **Penalty/fee for missed appointments**
6. **Ride-share/transportation partnerships** (Uber Health etc.)
7. **Patient portal / app with appointment management**

**Banned mechanism classes:** any solution whose core mechanism is (a) *reminding the patient closer to the appointment*, (b) *punishing/fee-ing the patient*, (c) *statistically overbooking to absorb loss*, (d) *removing transportation friction via ride services*, or (e) *adding a digital self-service portal*. No later candidate may rely on these as its primary lever, even if reframed (e.g., "smarter AI-personalized SMS timing" is still banned — same mechanism, thin disguise).

# DIVERGE

## Frame: independent dimensions

1. **Mechanism** — what actually changes behavior (info, social, financial, physical, structural/scheduling, identity)
2. **Actor** — who acts (patient, clinic staff, community member, algorithm, third party/employer)
3. **Who pays / bears cost** — hospital, patient, insurer, employer, community, no one (redesigned away)
4. **Timing** — at booking, days before, hours before, at the missed moment, after the fact, or pre-emptively at the *previous* visit
5. **What's removed vs. added** — subtract a step, invert an incentive, remove the concept of "the appointment" itself
6. **Unit of intervention** — individual patient, household, neighborhood/cohort, the slot itself, the clinic's identity/brand

## Candidates (short titles, spread across dimensions)

- **A. Booking-time commitment device** — patient picks their own reminder ritual/pledge at scheduling (actor=patient, timing=booking)
- **B. Buddy-slot pairing** — two patients from same neighborhood booked adjacently, encouraged to travel together (actor=community, mechanism=social)
- **C. Staff callback for high-risk slots only** — predictive model flags top-decile no-show risk, human (not bot) calls those specific patients (mechanism=social/human, unit=individual, algorithm-assisted triage)
- **D. Show-up lottery** — small raffle entries for attendance, funded by savings from reduced no-shows (mechanism=financial but *positive*, inverted from penalty)
- **E. "Last visit" seed — book the next appointment face-to-face before leaving, with the clinician (not scheduler) naming why it matters** (timing=previous visit, actor=clinician identity)
- **F. Open-slot marketplace** — patients can trade/release their slot to a visible community waitlist (like a standby list), removing the binary "show/no-show" into "show, release, or swap" (mechanism=structural, removes the failure mode itself)
- **G. Neighborhood health kiosk pre-check-in** — physical kiosk at pharmacy/grocery/church allows patients to "check in" for tomorrow's appointment in person, converting a passive reminder into an active micro-commitment 24h out
- **H. Clinic identity/reputation nudge** — patients told their specific clinician (not "the clinic") is expecting them; personalization at the *identity* level, not message level
- **I. Default-flip: appointment is auto-cancelled unless patient does one small action** (invert default — normally the default is "you're booked," flip so passive path = loss, forcing an active micro-yes)
- **J. Household unit scheduling** — book whole household's appointments same day/window, coordinating around one shared trip (unit=household, not individual)

## Borrow from 2 unrelated domains

**Domain 1: Restaurant reservation systems (OpenTable/Resy)**
Atomic rules:
- Rule 1: A credit card hold is taken but only charged on no-show *above a grace threshold of lateness*.
- Rule 2: Waitlists are public/visible — you can see how many people want your table.
- Rule 3: Popular restaurants build scarcity cues ("3 people booked this in the last hour") to increase perceived commitment value.

**Domain 2: Airline overbooking/standby & gate-boarding**
Atomic rules:
- Rule 1: Boarding happens in *called groups*, not free-for-all — the customer must respond to a call to remain in status.
- Rule 2: Standby passengers are ranked and visibly notified of their position, creating urgency.
- Rule 3: Denied boarding triggers immediate compensation *at the gate*, not after the fact — real-time feedback loop.

**Borrowed-mechanism candidates (borrowing must be load-bearing, not decorative):**

- **K. Visible community waitlist counter** (borrow Restaurant Rule 2/3) — when booking, patient sees "4 people are waiting for a slot this week" — converts an abstract missed slot into a visible cost to a *named neighbor group*, not the hospital. This is different from F (marketplace) because the mechanism here is purely *social visibility*, no actual trading feature needed — cheap to build.
- **L. Boarding-call check-in window** (borrow Airline Rule 1) — patient must "check in" within a live 2-hour window the day before (like online check-in), and if they don't, their slot auto-releases to standby *immediately*, not at appointment time — converts a silent no-show into an active status patients must maintain, shifting the failure to visible "you lost your spot" rather than invisible non-arrival.
- **M. Real-time gate compensation inversion** (borrow Airline Rule 3) — flip it: patients who show up *when someone else no-shows and get seen early* get a small real-time reward (fast-tracked, small voucher) — reinforcing that punctuality/standby-readiness pays off immediately, building a norm rather than deterring a single act.

# CONVERGE

## Critique each survivor

**A — Commitment device at booking**
- Must-hold assumption: patients have enough working memory/investment at booking time for a pledge to still bind weeks later.
- Cheapest falsifier: check if no-show rate correlates with *lead time* between booking and appointment — if long-lead-time bookings no-show just as much regardless of pledge, the pledge decayed.
- Why obvious ideas missed it: reminder-based thinking assumes the failure is *forgetting*; this assumes the failure is *decaying commitment*, a different psychological mechanism.

**C — Human callback, risk-triaged**
- Must-hold: a predictive model can actually identify a small high-risk decile with good precision using existing EHR/scheduling data.
- Falsifier: pull historical no-show data and check if simple features (past no-show count, appointment type, lead time) separate a top-decile group with >2x baseline no-show rate. If not separable, this collapses to "call everyone," which is just C's disguised twin of the banned IVR idea.
- Why missed: generalists jump straight to "automate reminders for all," skipping the cheaper insight that most of the problem is concentrated in a small subgroup — solved by triage, not scale.

**D — Show-up lottery**
- Must-hold: patients in this population respond more to small-probability upside than to guaranteed downside (loss aversion is *not* dominant here, contra usual assumption).
- Falsifier: a quick framing survey/A-B on reminder text ("you could win $50 for showing up" vs. control) over 2-4 weeks.
- Why missed: penalty-fee is the default first idea because "loss aversion" is the pop-behavioral-econ cliché; testing the inverse is rarely considered.

**E/H — Clinician-anchored booking**
- Must-hold: the *clinician* (not the front desk/scheduler) is a stronger commitment anchor than any external message.
- Falsifier: compare no-show rates for appointments booked by the clinician face-to-face at prior visit vs. booked by front-desk/phone for same patient population.
- Why missed: assistants default to "the system/message" as the actor; they underweight relational authority as a lever because it's harder to automate, so it's less salient to suggest.

**G — Neighborhood kiosk pre-check-in**
- Must-hold: proximity/physical routine (passing a kiosk at a pharmacy/grocery they already visit) creates a stronger cue than a phone notification.
- Falsifier: pilot in 2-3 high-no-show-rate neighborhoods with kiosks at high-foot-traffic third-party sites, measure check-in rate vs. matched control neighborhoods without kiosks.
- Why missed: bias toward digital-first solutions in an AI-generated list; physical/environmental cues are systematically underrepresented.

**J — Household unit scheduling**
- Must-hold: a meaningful share of no-shows stem from transportation/logistics coordination *failure across a household*, not individual forgetting.
- Falsifier: survey no-show patients (or a sample) on whether "someone else in household also had appointments that week" and whether trip-bundling would have helped.
- Why missed: scheduling systems are built per-patient by default; multi-person optimization is an engineering lift most generalists don't consider structurally.

**K/F — Visible social-cost waitlist**
- Must-hold: patients care about a *visible, specific* other person/group losing a slot more than an abstract "please don't no-show" ask.
- Falsifier: A/B test booking confirmation copy: neutral vs. "X other patients are waiting for this time slot" — measure no-show delta over a month.
- Why missed: generalists treat the appointment as a private transaction between patient and clinic; reframing it as a shared/scarce community resource is a structural inversion, not a messaging tweak.

**L/I — Boarding-call check-in window**
- Must-hold: patients who fail to actively check in within a live window are meaningfully different (more likely to no-show) than those who do, i.e., the check-in itself is diagnostic and actionable in time to backfill the slot.
- Falsifier: run a shadow pilot — send the check-in prompt but don't actually release slots yet; measure what fraction fail to check in, and whether "failed to check in" predicts actual no-show with enough lead time to matter.
- Why missed: assistants think of no-show as an *endpoint* to prevent; this treats it as a *status* that can be monitored and intervened on before it happens, structurally borrowed from a domain where standing capacity recovery is core business.

**M — Real-time standby reward**
- Must-hold: enough standby-eligible patients exist near the hospital, and slot-release-to-arrival latency can be under ~30-60 min for the reward to be deliverable same-day.
- Falsifier: check historical same-day cancellation timing distribution — if most cancellations happen the night before (not hours before), there's no real-time window to reward into.
- Why missed: this requires operational infrastructure (real-time slot release + notification) that's a bigger lift, so it's typically skipped in favor of static reminders — but it directly recovers utilization rather than just reducing the no-show number.

## Pairwise knockout (most likely to matter for THIS brief: public hospital chain, likely resource-constrained, large heterogeneous patient population)

- **C vs. A**: C wins — triage is operationally cheaper (no new patient-facing ritual to design) and targets where the actual loss is concentrated.
- **C vs. G**: G wins on population fit if the hospital serves low-digital-access populations (public hospital chain implies this), but C is cheaper to pilot first. Keep both — different cost/reach tradeoffs.
- **K/F vs. D**: K wins — cheaper to test (a copy change vs. a lottery-funding mechanism), and social-visibility effects tend to be more durable than one-off financial gamification which fatigues.
- **K/F vs. C**: different mechanisms, not substitutes — C is human-triage-based, K is structural/self-service. Both survive as complements.
- **L/I vs. M**: L is the prerequisite for M (you need real-time check-in status before you can do real-time backfill reward) — L wins standalone; M is a valuable *extension* if L works.
- **E/H vs. C**: E/H requires clinician behavior change (harder to scale, depends on clinician buy-in across a chain); C is scriptable/algorithmic and easier to roll out uniformly across a multi-site chain. C wins for feasibility at *this* scale (a chain, not a single clinic).
- **J vs. G**: G wins — J requires deep scheduling-system rework (cross-patient coordination logic) with uncertain payoff; G is a low-tech pilot addressable in weeks.

**Final ranking of survivors for this brief: C > K/F > G > L/I > (E/H, D, A, J, M as second-tier/complementary)**

# RECOMMENDATION

## Best practical idea: **C — Risk-triaged human callback**
**Non-obvious insight it depends on:** No-show risk in a hospital population is not uniform — a small subgroup (likely 15-25% of scheduled patients, based on typical clinic distributions) probably accounts for the majority of no-shows, and *that* subgroup responds to human relational contact, not automated reminders (which is why blanket SMS programs plateau at modest gains). The intervention that scales is triage, not communication channel.
**First cheap test:** Pull 6-12 months of scheduling/EHR data, build a simple logistic model (past no-shows, appointment type, lead time, day of week) and check whether it identifies a top-decile group with substantially elevated no-show rate (>2x baseline). If yes, pilot human callbacks *only* for that decile at one site for one month, compare to a matched control site running standard reminders. This costs a data pull and a few staff-hours, not a system build.

## Best wildcard idea: **K/F — Visible social-cost waitlist**
**Non-obvious insight it depends on:** Patients currently experience their appointment as a private, low-stakes transaction with an anonymous institution — the *decision to no-show* has no visible social cost. Making the scarcity and the waiting patients visible at the moment of booking or reminder reframes the appointment as a shared resource, borrowing the same psychological lever that makes restaurant waitlists and public commitments effective, without requiring any financial mechanism, new staff time, or new physical infrastructure.
**First cheap test:** A/B test on the existing confirmation SMS/portal copy alone — control message vs. one line added ("3 other patients are hoping for this time slot if you can't make it — let us know early so we can offer it to them"). Run for 4 weeks across matched clinics, compare no-show rates. This is a copy change, deployable in days, with no new tooling.
