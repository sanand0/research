# IDEATE

## Step 1: 5 people who'd see "reading a book" differently

**A blind musician** — reading is already non-visual; thinks in time, rhythm, texture.
1. A novel as a listenable album where chapter transitions are key changes, not silence.
2. Braille that changes texture based on narrative tension (raised dots get sharper in suspense).
3. A book you "read" by learning to play it on an instrument — plot progresses as you master a piece.

**A cartographer** — thinks in space, scale, overlapping layers, not linear sequence.
1. A book as a physical territory you walk through (AR), where re-reading means taking a different path.
2. A novel printed as a single giant map-poster; "reading order" is a route you choose, like a subway map.
3. Marginalia as elevation lines — density of past readers' notes creates visible "terrain" you read through.

**A four-year-old** — no fixed attention span, wants agency, doesn't distinguish reading from playing.
1. A book that only advances when you act out the next line physically.
2. A story that gets reread each night but subtly punishes/rewards yesterday's choices.
3. A book you "feed" — literally posting pages into a slot triggers the next page appearing.

**A dying person / hospice patient** — time is scarce, wants presence not plot, may not finish anything again.
1. A book designed to be readable in any single sitting and still feel "complete," regardless of where you stop.
2. A book that is read TO you by someone you love, with a script for their voice, not a narrator.
3. A "last chapter first" format — you always start knowing the ending, so nothing is left unresolved if you stop.

**A compiler/software engineer** — thinks in dependencies, versioning, diffs, not fixed final text.
1. A book with a public "changelog" — you read the diff between edition 1.0 and 4.0 as the content itself.
2. A novel that forks into git-branch storylines; readers merge branches by voting.
3. A book that only reveals a paragraph once you've "resolved" a contradiction in the previous one (like fixing a bug before compiling).

## Step 2: 2 unrelated domains → 3 atomic rules each → new ideas

**Domain: Composting / decomposition**
Rules: (1) inputs break down at different rates, (2) the process needs a mix of "green" and "brown" material to work, (3) end product feeds something new, it isn't the final goal.
Ideas:
- A book printed on seed paper where finishing a chapter means literally burying and planting it — you grow the next chapter's setting as a plant, "reading" its growth over weeks.
- A "compost edition": read fast plot pages (green) interleaved with slow essayistic pages (brown); ratio determines how well the book "digests" in memory (tested via spaced recall app).
- A book that explicitly exists to be broken down into notes, and the "finished" state is a compost heap of reader annotations that becomes raw material for the next reader's edition.

**Domain: Air traffic control**
Rules: (1) many objects moving simultaneously must be sequenced by a controller, not by their own preference, (2) near-misses are the moments of highest information value, (3) handoffs occur between zones with different rules.
Ideas:
- A multiplayer book where each reader is assigned one character's chapters in real time; a "control" layer sequences whose chapter you get next based on where other readers currently are, creating live near-miss collisions of plotlines.
- A book that flags its own "near misses" — moments where the plot almost went differently — as a separate readable track you can toggle into, like switching radio frequencies.
- A book divided into "airspace zones" (tone/POV) with explicit handoff pages that change the reading rules (font, pace, even physical orientation of the page) as you cross a zone boundary.

# CONVERGE (banning the obvious)

**5 most conventional ideas to ban** (and near-duplicates): audiobooks/narration-as-is, "choose your own adventure" branching, AR overlay walking tours, annotated/marginalia editions, book club discussion apps. I'll exclude straightforward versions of these from the surviving set.

**Surviving, deduplicated, diverse set (final selection):**

1. **Compost Chapter** — bury a printed chapter (seed paper), grow next chapter's setting as a plant; reading pace = biological time.
2. **Last Chapter First** — hospice-inspired: you always read the ending first, then wander backward at will; nothing is left "unresolved" by stopping.
3. **Read-to-you Script** — a book formatted as a script for a specific loved one to read aloud to you, with blank prompts for personalization (not narration — relationship).
4. **Git-Branch Novel** — story literally versioned; readers "merge" branches via consensus voting, and reading = reviewing a diff, not a fixed text.
5. **ATC Handoff Book** — physical/formal rules of reading change at zone-boundary pages (rotate the book, switch font/rhythm) as POV or tone shifts.
6. **Live Multiplayer Sequencing** — a "controller" assigns which character's chapter you get next based on real-time position of other readers, producing emergent near-misses between plotlines.
7. **Near-Miss Track** — parallel thin channel of "almost-happened" scenes, toggled into like changing a radio dial, read alongside the main text.
8. **Instrument-Learned Novel** — plot advances as you learn to physically play a piece of music; mastery unlocks pages.
9. **Texture-Tension Braille** — braille whose physical raised texture sharpens/softens with narrative tension, independent of translation into words.
10. **Terrain-of-Notes Edition** — a book whose page background is literally shaped (embossed/printed) by the accumulated density of prior readers' marginalia — you read the crowd before you read the text.

# Recommendation

**Best practical idea: #3, Read-to-you Script.**
Non-obvious insight: we've optimized audiobooks for *efficiency* of narration, but the actual desire in many "read to me" contexts (parents, hospice, long-distance partners) isn't a good narrator — it's a specific beloved voice, imperfect and personal. A book formatted explicitly as a two-person script — with stage directions, blanks for names, pacing notes for *that* relationship — turns reading into a bonding ritual instead of content delivery. It's buildable now (just a formatting/editorial innovation), commercially testable in a niche (children's books, hospice/palliative libraries, long-distance-relationship gifting), and needs zero new tech.

**Best wildcard idea: #6, Live Multiplayer Sequencing (ATC-style reading).**
Non-obvious insight: reading has always been treated as something you do in isolation, then discuss after — like patients in separate rooms comparing notes post-op. Air traffic control's insight is that many independent actors moving through the same space *at the same time* generates real information (near-misses, tension, sequencing decisions) that doesn't exist if they move separately. Applying this to a novel means the *order* in which chapters are released to you depends on strangers reading concurrently — the book becomes a live event with real scarcity and real "traffic," not a fixed artifact. It's the hardest to build and has a cold-start problem, but it's the one idea that couldn't have existed as a metaphor before real-time networked devices — it's structurally new, not just a new packaging of an old form.
