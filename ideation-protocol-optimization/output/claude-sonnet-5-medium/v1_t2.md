# IDEATE

## Step 1: 5 people who'd see this differently, with candidate ideas

**1. A behavioral economist**
- Commitment device: patient handwrites their own appointment card (implementation intention effect)
- Reframe reminders from "your appointment is on X" to loss-framed "you'll lose your slot"
- Deposit-refund micro-incentive (small refundable stake, waived for low-income)

**2. A long-haul truck dispatcher**
- Treat appointment slots like freight capacity — overbook probabilistically based on route (patient) history
- "Standby lane" — a rolling waitlist that texts the next patient the moment a slot opens, like standby boarding
- Dynamic slot bundling: group high-no-show-risk patients into flexible block times instead of fixed slots

**3. A grandmother who runs the neighborhood WhatsApp group**
- Peer buddy system — pair patients going to the same clinic day so they remind/accompany each other
- Community elder/auntie makes personal calls the night before (trust > SMS)
- Appointment tied to a social event (e.g., clinic day = market day, so the trip is "already happening")

**4. A video game designer**
- Streak/attendance reward system (gamified adherence, not for health outcomes but for showing up)
- Uncertainty/lootbox mechanic: small surprise reward for attendance (variable reward schedules are sticky)
- Progress bar UI showing "care journey completion %" so missing feels like abandoning a save file

**5. A supply chain / airline revenue manager**
- Predictive no-show scoring (like flight overbooking models) to dynamically overbook risky slots
- Dynamic pricing analog: shorter-notice or higher-friction slots go to low-risk patients; high-risk patients get high-touch slots
- "Fare class" tiers — patients who confirm 48 hrs ahead get priority/perks; unconfirmed slots auto-release

## Step 2: 2 unrelated domains → atomic rules → more ideas

**Domain A: Restaurant reservations (OpenTable/Resy)**
Rules:
1. Multi-touch confirmation cascade (booking → 24h → 2h reminders, each requiring reply)
2. No-show tracking follows the *person*, not the venue, and affects future booking privileges
3. Waitlists convert idle capacity into filled capacity in real time

→ Ideas: cross-hospital no-show reputation score (portable, not punitive but informative); real-time slot-release-to-waitlist SMS; "confirm or lose it" 2-hour final call with instant reallocation.

**Domain B: Crop irrigation / agricultural scheduling**
Rules:
1. Systems are built around unpredictable externalities (weather) rather than fighting them
2. Buffer zones and slack absorb variance instead of trying to eliminate it
3. Local micro-conditions (soil, sub-plot) determine timing, not a central uniform schedule

→ Ideas: build slack/buffer slots into every clinic day sized to that clinic's historical variance; decentralize scheduling to community health workers who know local "micro-conditions" (paydays, transport, weather, harvest season) instead of central booking; treat transport/weather disruption as an expected input and auto-reschedule around it rather than penalizing patient.

## Step 3: Ban the 5 most obvious/conventional ideas

Banning (and near-duplicates):
1. SMS/text reminders — already standard everywhere
2. Phone call reminders — standard
3. Overbooking based on no-show risk — standard airline-style, well-trodden in healthcare ops research
4. Reducing wait times / improving clinic experience — generic, not a specific mechanism
5. Financial penalties/fines for no-shows — common, ethically fraught, well-covered

Cut from the candidate list above: plain SMS/call reminders (dispatcher's texting elements survive only if given a novel twist), basic overbooking (survives only via the dynamic/portable-score variant), fines (deposit-refund is a variant but must be reframed to survive—kept as opt-in gamified deposit, not punitive).

## Recommendations

### Best practical idea: **#8/18 — Anchor scheduling to existing errands/community rhythms**, paired with **#14 — structural slack sized to per-clinic historical variance**

**Non-obvious insight:** No-shows are usually modeled as a *motivation* or *communication* failure (hence the industry's obsession with reminders). The irrigation-domain reframe treats absence as a **structural/logistical variance problem**: people skip appointments largely because of transport cost/friction and unpredictable life events, not forgetfulness. Two moves follow: (a) schedule appointments to coincide with days patients are *already* traveling for other reasons (market day, paydays, school pickup), cutting the marginal cost of attending to near zero; (b) instead of fighting variance with penalties, size buffer capacity into each clinic's day the way an irrigation system sizes buffer zones to known rainfall variance — a no-show stops being "wasted capacity" and becomes an expected, absorbed cost. This is practical because it needs no new technology, just rescheduling logic and community-calendar data the hospital chain can gather with existing staff.

### Best wildcard idea: **#15 — Decentralized, community-health-worker-driven scheduling**

**Non-obvious insight:** Centralized hospital scheduling optimizes for the institution's calendar, not the patient's life. Borrowing the agricultural principle that *local micro-conditions* (soil type, sub-plot moisture) should drive timing rather than a uniform regional schedule, this idea pushes scheduling authority out to community health workers who have tacit, hyper-local knowledge (this family only has transport on Thursdays, this neighborhood floods in monsoon season, this patient's employer pays weekly on Fridays making Friday appointments impossible). It's a wildcard because it requires organizational restructuring — giving up centralized control of the calendar — but it attacks the actual root cause (a mismatch between institutional time and lived time) rather than symptom-patching with reminders, and it compounds: CHWs also build the trust that "elder pre-call" (#7) tries to buy from scratch.

**Second wildcard (cheap to test in parallel):** #16 — auto-reschedule triggered by external disruption signals (weather, transit strikes). It's a low-cost pilot (integrate a weather/transit API with the scheduling system) that removes blame from the patient for a real, non-obvious share of no-shows, and could be A/B tested independently of the larger organizational changes above.
