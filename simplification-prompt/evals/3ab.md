### A-only

* At 15% monthly growth, revenue would approximately double in five months.
* The company should explicitly extend runway to **9–12 months** through selective cuts.
* Cuts should target low-return marketing, speculative hiring, excess tooling, and projects unrelated to retention or near-term revenue.
* Product reliability, customer success, and proven acquisition channels should be protected.
* Churn should be separated into voluntary churn versus failed payments, and logo churn versus revenue churn.
* Losing many small, unprofitable customers could make higher churn less alarming.
* Growth driven by discounts or paid acquisition is a reason to cut more deeply.
* The full fundraising process might be delayed for two or three months when churn has a known, rapidly fixable cause, while preparation continues immediately.
* The final decision rule is whether each cohort generates enough retained gross profit, after acquisition and service costs, soon enough to survive the runway.

### B-only

* Churn may have doubled because of one large customer, a product problem, a price change, or poor-quality customers.
* Burn multiple is named as an important metric.
* Investors may postpone investing until retention improves, rather than merely lowering valuation.
* Weak fundraising market conditions are an explicit reason to cut more strongly.
* Faster growth is more defensible when new customers retain better than older customers.
* Each new customer increasing the company’s cash loss is an explicit stopping condition.

### Per-criterion winners

1. **Correctness — Tie.** Both give a financially and operationally sound combined recommendation, and neither contains a material factual error.

2. **Key drivers — A.** A makes the central decision variable retained cohort gross profit relative to acquisition and servicing cost within the remaining runway; B names the right metrics but does not integrate them into as precise a decision test.

3. **Mechanism — A.** A more fully explains how headline growth can be produced by repeatedly replacing churned customers, why fundraising delay reduces leverage, and why cohort retention determines whether growth creates durable value.

4. **Caveats — A.** A distinguishes benign churn from dangerous churn, including unprofitable small customers, failed payments, logo versus revenue churn, and a narrowly defined case for briefly delaying the formal raise.

5. **Calibration — A.** A recommends an asymmetric default while clearly limiting aggressive growth and fundraising delay to specific evidence; it avoids treating either the 15% growth or doubled churn as decisive by itself.

6. **Actionability — A.** A supplies a target runway, specific costs to remove or protect, detailed churn analyses, and measurable conditions for changing course. B is also actionable but less specific about execution thresholds.
