Do all three, but asymmetrically: **raise now, cut non-core burn immediately, and keep funding growth only where retention-adjusted economics are strong.**

With six months of runway, waiting is risky. At 15% monthly growth, revenue could roughly double over five months, but doubled churn may mean the company is buying temporary revenue rather than building a durable base.

| Option                 | Benefit                                    | Main risk                                                     |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cut costs hard         | Extends runway and negotiating leverage    | Slows growth, damages morale, may make fundraising harder     |
| Raise now              | Avoids a cash crisis and preserves options | Weak churn metrics reduce valuation and investor confidence   |
| Keep growing unchanged | Maximizes headline revenue momentum        | Can accelerate cash burn while concealing a retention problem |

The default plan should be:

1. **Start fundraising immediately.** Six months is not much buffer once diligence, negotiations, and unexpected delays are included. Present the raise as financing a credible path to efficient growth—not as emergency capital.

2. **Extend runway to at least 9–12 months through selective cuts.** Remove low-ROI marketing, speculative hiring, excessive tooling, and projects unrelated to retention or near-term revenue. Avoid indiscriminate cuts to product reliability, customer success, or proven acquisition channels.

3. **Treat churn as the primary operating emergency.** Break it down by customer cohort, segment, acquisition channel, contract type, and churn reason. Separate voluntary churn from failed payments and distinguish logo churn from revenue churn.

4. **Continue only retention-adjusted growth.** A channel that produces rapid sign-ups but poor retained revenue is not genuinely growing the business. Shift spending toward cohorts with strong payback and retention.

What would change the answer:

* **Raise more aggressively** if net revenue retention remains strong, churn increased because many tiny or unprofitable customers left, gross margins are healthy, and the company has repeatable acquisition economics.
* **Cut more deeply** if churn is broad-based, recent cohorts are deteriorating, customer acquisition payback exceeds the remaining runway, or growth depends heavily on discounts and paid acquisition.
* **Delay the full fundraising process briefly—but not preparation** if churn has a clearly identified, rapidly fixable cause and two or three months of data would materially improve the financing outcome.
* **Keep growing aggressively** only if the company has high confidence that churn is temporary, sufficient cash access, strong contribution margins, and evidence that retained cohorts produce attractive lifetime value.

The decisive metric is not 15% monthly revenue growth by itself. It is whether **retained gross profit from each new cohort exceeds the fully loaded cost of acquiring and serving it, quickly enough to survive the runway**.
