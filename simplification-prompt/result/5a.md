## The belief most likely wrong

**“On a representative task set, an agent’s end-to-end success rate—especially when judged by humans—is the best proxy for its production value.”**

The error is treating task success as a sufficient statistic. For most enterprise agents, the economically relevant unit is not a successful output but **human work actually eliminated**.

A better primary metric is:

[
\text{Autonomous surplus}
=========================

## \text{value of accepted work}

## \text{review, repair, clarification, handoff, and rework}

## \text{compute/tool cost}

\text{expected failure loss}
]

Success rate remains an important diagnostic, but it should not be the selection objective.

### Why this qualifies as a widely held belief

The clearest evidence is a 2025–26 study of 306 agent practitioners, including 86 deployed or pilot systems and 20 detailed company cases. Productivity and reduced human effort were the dominant reasons for deployment. Yet evaluation centred on whether outputs were correct and high-quality: 74% primarily used human evaluation, while only 38.7% compared their agent against a human or non-agentic baseline. In other words, companies frequently optimize output quality while claiming human-time savings as the business outcome. ([arXiv][1])

## Primary evidence against it

**1. High perceived and benchmark capability can coexist with negative productivity.**

METR randomized 246 real tasks performed by 16 experienced maintainers working on repositories they knew well. Developers forecast a 24% speed-up and, after the experiment, believed AI had made them 20% faster. Measured completion time instead increased by 19%. The study explicitly contrasts this result with strong benchmark performance. This is unusually direct evidence that acceptable-looking agent contributions can impose interaction, checking and correction costs that success-oriented evaluation misses. ([arXiv][2])

This does **not** establish that coding agents generally reduce productivity; the setting involved experienced maintainers, mature repositories and early-2025 tools. It does establish that neither benchmark success nor users’ retrospective assessment is a safe proxy for net work eliminated.

**2. Passing the objective grader often leaves substantial hidden work.**

In an initial METR study, a coding agent passed maintainer-written tests on 38% of tasks, but none of 15 manually reviewed pull requests was mergeable as submitted. Even test-passing submissions required an estimated 26 minutes of repair on average—about one-third of the original human task time. ([Metr][3])

A larger follow-up had four active repository maintainers blindly assess 296 AI-generated patches. Roughly half of patches that passed SWE-bench Verified’s automated grader would not have been merged; maintainer acceptance was about 24 percentage points below automated scores after normalization against human patches. ([Metr][4])

The important variable was not merely correctness. It was **distance from usable completion**.

**3. Production failures predominantly manifest as human effort, not catastrophic visible errors.**

An observational analysis of 20,574 real coding-agent sessions across 1,639 repositories found that 90.5% of observed misalignment episodes imposed effort or trust costs rather than irreversible damage. Of episodes with visible resolutions, 91.49% required explicit user correction. Those costs can be almost invisible in an eval that inspects only the final artifact or asks whether the task eventually succeeded. ([arXiv][5])

**4. Even production-grounded aggregate scores can choose the economically inferior system.**

AlphaEval used 94 tasks sourced from seven companies across six professional domains. It found large variation by domain and agent scaffold, and showed cases where average-score rankings differed from economically weighted value rankings. The authors also identified production-specific failures such as outputs that were substantively reasonable but unusable by downstream systems because of formatting or integration requirements. ([arXiv][6])

Collectively, the evidence suggests that **verification and integration burden is not noise around task success; it is often the principal cost of using the agent.**

## Strongest innocent alternative

The best alternative explanation is:

> Task success is the correct metric; companies merely use unrepresentative, overly clean benchmarks.

There is real evidence for this. Nubank reports five customer-support agent deployments using production-derived simulations, calibrated LLM judges and online A/B tests. Offline evaluation correlated strongly with online outcomes; one deployment improved transactional NPS by 37 percentage points and self-service rate by 29 percentage points over earlier variants. ([arXiv][7])

This rules out the stronger claim that offline evaluation or task-quality scoring is inherently useless. A well-calibrated, production-derived evaluation can be highly predictive.

But Nubank’s evidence does not rescue **raw success rate as the sufficient metric**. Its online validation explicitly included:

* self-service rate, which measures whether human handling was avoided;
* customer satisfaction across the entire journey;
* graceful human handoff;
* fallback and evaluator-failure rates.

Those are burden- and workflow-aware outcomes, not merely “did the agent eventually produce a correct answer?” ([arXiv][7])

The most defensible synthesis is therefore:

**Representative tasks solve distribution shift. They do not automatically solve objective mismatch.**
Production-grounded success rate works when its rubric or validation is tightly coupled to human effort, handoffs and business outcomes.

## Cheapest decisive falsification test

Use an existing randomized rollout if available; otherwise run this in shadow mode on the next **100 representative cases**.

Choose three or four already-available agent configurations—for example the current agent, a cheaper model, an aggressive always-attempt policy and a conservative fail-fast policy. Randomly assign configurations to cases.

For each case, collect two measurements:

1. **Conventional evaluation:** a blinded expert marks the final result pass/fail using the company’s current rubric.
2. **Workflow evaluation:** an operator actually takes the result to completion while the system records:

   * active review and repair minutes;
   * clarifications and interventions;
   * handoff or escalation;
   * reopened or reversed work;
   * model and tool cost;
   * final quality or downstream acceptance.

Use matched historical human-only cases—or a 20-case human-only subsample—to estimate baseline effort. Calculate net human minutes saved at the required quality level.

Pre-register this falsifier:

> **My conclusion is false for this workflow if the configuration selected by raw pass rate is also the configuration with the highest net value, and including review, repair and handoff variables neither changes the selection nor improves held-out prediction of net value by a practically meaningful amount—say, more than 10%.**

This test is cheap because it requires no new benchmark, model training or risky autonomous deployment. It reuses real tasks, existing configurations and ordinary operators. A hundred shadow cases are usually sufficient to reveal a large ranking reversal; smaller differences can be followed with sequential sampling.

## Bottom line

The likely mistake is **evaluating the agent as a producer of answers rather than as a substitute for a workflow**.

The key question is not:

> “How often does it finish correctly?”

It is:

> **“How much valuable work remains removed after humans verify, repair, coordinate and absorb its failures?”**

My confidence is moderate rather than absolute: the strongest causal evidence is concentrated in software work, while the broad cross-industry evidence is partly survey-based or observational. The Nubank deployment demonstrates that carefully production-calibrated offline evaluation can work extremely well. But it works precisely because it closes the loop to human handoff, customer experience and real operational outcomes—not because terminal task success is universally sufficient.
