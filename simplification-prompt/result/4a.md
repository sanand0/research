## Smallest reliable design: randomized, blinded, within-person crossover

Use **24–30 employees**, each making **12–16 realistic decisions** drawn from a validated case bank. Each employee completes half with AI suggestions and half without, in randomized order. This yields roughly **300–400 decisions** while controlling for large differences in employee skill.

### Control

**Treatment:** Employee receives the AI suggestion, rationale, and supporting evidence.

**Control:** Same task, data, interface, and permitted reference materials, but no AI-generated recommendation. A neutral panel can occupy the same screen space to reduce interface and attention effects.

Randomize at the **decision level**, not the employee level. Use matched case pairs of similar difficulty and counterbalance them so that no employee sees the same case twice.

Keep the time allowance identical in both conditions. Employees may finish early, but quality is evaluated independently of completion time.

### Primary metric

Use a preregistered **decision-quality score**, assigned by evaluators who do not know whether AI was used.

A practical 0–10 rubric:

* Correct action or recommendation: 0–4
* Appropriate use of evidence: 0–2
* Identification of material risks: 0–2
* Reasoning consistency: 0–1
* Appropriate uncertainty or escalation: 0–1

Have two independent experts score at least 25% of responses. Require acceptable inter-rater reliability before analyzing treatment effects.

The estimand is:

> Average within-employee difference in quality between AI-assisted and control decisions.

Analyze it with a mixed-effects model containing employee and case as random effects.

### Secondary metrics

Measure these separately; do not combine them with the primary outcome:

* Rate of materially harmful or policy-violating decisions
* Confidence calibration: whether confidence tracks correctness
* Appropriate escalation or abstention
* Acceptance of incorrect AI suggestions
* Decision time
* Employee confidence and perceived usefulness

The experiment succeeds only on quality. Faster decisions with unchanged or worse quality do not count as improvement.

### Essential stress test

Include approximately **20% adversarial cases** where the AI suggestion is plausible but wrong. Employees should not know which cases these are.

This distinguishes genuine decision support from automation bias. Report treatment effects separately for:

1. AI-correct cases
2. AI-wrong cases
3. Ambiguous cases where escalation is appropriate

### Main confounds and controls

| Confound                                  | Control                                                              |
| ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Employee skill differences                | Within-person randomization                                          |
| Unequal case difficulty                   | Matched cases; case random effects                                   |
| Learning and fatigue                      | Randomized order; counterbalanced treatment sequence                 |
| AI condition receiving more information   | Give both groups identical source material                           |
| Evaluator expectations                    | Blind evaluators; anonymized responses                               |
| Speed–quality trade-off                   | Fixed maximum time; quality as primary outcome                       |
| Novelty or Hawthorne effects              | Short practice round in both conditions                              |
| AI occasionally being unavailable or slow | Log failures; analyze intention-to-treat                             |
| Employees recognizing benchmark answers   | Use unfamiliar, organization-specific cases                          |
| Collaboration or answer leakage           | Individual sessions or isolated case delivery                        |
| Selective AI adoption                     | Log whether suggestions were viewed, accepted, modified, or rejected |
| Outcome cherry-picking                    | Preregister one primary metric and analysis                          |

### Stopping rule

Define a **smallest worthwhile improvement** before starting—for example, **0.25 rubric points on a 10-point scale**, or **0.20 standard deviations**.

Run one interim analysis after half the planned decisions and stop only under these preregistered conditions:

* **Success:** The confidence interval’s lower bound exceeds the smallest worthwhile improvement, and there is no meaningful increase in harmful decisions.
* **Futility:** The confidence interval’s upper bound is below the smallest worthwhile improvement.
* **Otherwise:** Continue to the planned maximum of approximately 400 decisions.

Do not stop merely because `p < 0.05`. The result should demonstrate a practically useful quality improvement, not just evidence that the effect is nonzero.

### Decision rule

Recommend deployment only when all three conditions hold:

1. Blinded decision quality improves by at least the preregistered minimum.
2. Harmful-error rates do not increase.
3. Performance on deliberately wrong AI suggestions does not materially deteriorate.

This design is small because it uses repeated decisions from each employee, but reliable because treatment is randomized, scoring is blinded, case difficulty is controlled, and automation bias is tested directly.
