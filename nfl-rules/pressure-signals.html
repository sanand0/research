The Proposals That Won't Die

Eight rule proposals failed in the NFL's spring meetings. That's not the story. The story is what they tell us about where the game is under stress.

Analysis of 2023–2026 NFL proposal records  ·  Official NFL Operations data

In the spring of 2025, representatives from the Green Bay Packers stood before the owners of all 31 other NFL teams and made their case: that a specific play used primarily by the Philadelphia Eagles had become fundamentally unstoppable, and should be banned.

The play — sometimes called the "tush push" — involves the quarterback lining up directly behind the center, with eight or more teammates clustered just behind him. When the ball is snapped, those teammates push as a unit. Near the goal line, where a single yard can decide a game, the Eagles convert this play at a rate that borders on statistical impossibility to stop. Green Bay wanted it gone.

The vote went against them. Philadelphia's play survived.

Green Bay wasn't alone that spring. Detroit proposed removing the automatic first down that currently follows defensive holding and illegal contact penalties. Philadelphia had already tried, two years earlier, to replace the nearly obsolete onside kick with a 4th-and-20 offensive option. Cleveland wanted to extend the window for trading future draft picks from two years to five. All of them failed.

In the four-year span between 2023 and 2026, eight substantive rule proposals died without becoming law. They won't stay dead.

What Failed Proposals Actually Are

The temptation is to read a failed proposal as a rejection: the league looked at the idea, didn't like it, and moved on. That reading misses something important. A proposal doesn't appear out of nowhere. It requires a team's front office to believe strongly enough in a problem that they're willing to put their credibility on the line in front of every other team and the league's competition committee.

When a proposal fails, the problem doesn't disappear. The play that prompted Green Bay's proposal still exists. The playoff seeding imbalance that frustrated Detroit still exists. The onside kick still succeeds at roughly 10%. Failed proposals are sensors — pressure gauges on the parts of the game that teams experience as broken, even when they can't yet assemble a majority to fix them.

The Three You Need to Know

Score 9
The Tush Push — Green Bay, 2025
Proposer: Green Bay Packers  ·  Lineage: Offensive Strategy  ·  Outcome: Failed

For readers who don't watch football: near the goal line, the Philadelphia Eagles' quarterback Jalen Hurts lines up right behind his center and his teammates cluster directly behind him like a human engine. On the snap, they all push together. The result is a battering ram that most defenses have essentially given up trying to stop. The play converts near the goal line at rates that would be exceptional for any play in football, let alone a 1-yard sneak. Green Bay's proposal would have required all blockers to be on the line of scrimmage, effectively preventing the "push." The NFL voted it down. Hurts, for his part, credited his teammates' strength and technique. The play remains legal. The resentment is building.

Score 11
Seed Wild Cards Above Weaker Division Winners — Detroit, 2025
Proposer: Detroit Lions  ·  Lineage: Competitive Equity  ·  Outcome: Failed

Here's a scenario that happens in the NFL regularly: a team wins 12 games, doesn't win their division, and enters the playoffs as a "wild card." They have to play on the road — away from their home stadium — against a "division champion" that won only 9 or even 8 games. The 12-win team has a worse playoff seed than the 8-win team, purely because of division structure. Detroit's proposal would have let better-record teams leapfrog weaker division champions in playoff seeding. Simple. Logical. Vetoed. The league apparently values division titles more than record-based fairness. The pressure from teams that feel burned by this will continue.

Score 10
Replace the Onside Kick With a 4th-and-20 Option — Philadelphia, 2023
Proposer: Philadelphia Eagles  ·  Lineage: Kickoff Strategy  ·  Outcome: Failed

The onside kick — a surprise short kick that a trailing team hopes to recover — succeeds about 10% of the time in modern football. As the kickoff was redesigned for safety, the onside became even harder to execute. Philadelphia proposed giving trailing teams a new option: instead of a desperation onside kick, they could choose to run a 4th-and-20 offensive play from their own 35-yard line. Converting a 20-yard play is hard, but not 10% hard. It would give comebacks a fighting chance. The proposal hasn't passed, but the dynamic kickoff redesign has made the problem it was trying to solve even more acute — expect it back.

The Full Pressure Map

The other five failed proposals cover a range of pain points: a structural penalty that gives automatic first downs to defenses regardless of context (Detroit, again), a punt touchback rule that would move the starting spot from the 20 to the 25 (Competition Committee), the ability to trade draft picks five years into the future (Cleveland), a tightening of crackback block rules (New York Jets), and an exception to the 90-player roster limit for injured players (Detroit, again).

Notice something: Detroit proposed four of the eight failed signals in this dataset. That's not a coincidence — the Lions are one of the most activist teams in recent NFL governance, bringing specific, data-informed complaints about competitive imbalances. They've also won some battles: their proposal to expand the coach's challenge system passed in 2024. But their bigger structural reform proposals — playoff seeding, defensive contact penalties — haven't yet found a majority.

Move Punt Touchbacks to the 25-Yard Line — Competition Committee, 2023
Proposer: Competition Committee  ·  Lineage: Special Teams  ·  Score: 9

Currently, a punt that goes into the end zone (or is downed there) gives the receiving team the ball at their own 20-yard line. This proposal would move that to the 25. The logic: incentivize more punt returns by reducing the benefit of a touchback. The Competition Committee brought it themselves — a sign it reflects genuine interest — but didn't pass it. This is the most unusual item in the list: the group that almost never fails (see the next story) proposed something and declined to push it through.

Remove Automatic First Down for Defensive Holding — Detroit, 2025
Proposer: Detroit Lions  ·  Lineage: Offense-Defense Balance  ·  Score: 11

When a defender commits holding or illegal contact — two fairly common infractions — the offense currently gets an automatic first down, regardless of the original down and distance. Detroit proposed treating these as regular 5-yard penalties without the automatic first down. The argument: a 5-yard holding penalty in 3rd-and-20 shouldn't automatically give the offense a fresh set of downs. The automatic first down was designed as a strong deterrent, but Detroit thinks it has become disproportionate. The NFL disagreed. The imbalance remains.

How to Watch For These

A failed proposal that came from an individual club and later appears as a Competition Committee proposal is the clearest sign it has moved from "one team's complaint" to "league-recognized problem." Watch for the tush push, playoff seeding, and the 4th-and-20 option to reappear in that pattern. The data suggests all three are still accumulating pressure.

The NFL's rule-making process is, by design, conservative. Changes require a supermajority, and the Competition Committee — the most influential body in the process — tends to move deliberately. That's not a flaw. Large, complex institutions probably should require broad consensus before changing their fundamental rules. But it means that when a rule change finally happens, it's usually after years of failed proposals that accumulated enough pressure to force a decision. The proposals that won't die are the leading indicators.

Methodology. Pressure signals are proposed-only canonical rule changes (no captured approval) from the 2023–2026 official NFL Operations dataset.
