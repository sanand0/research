The Committee Always Wins

In four years of NFL rule-making, the Competition Committee proposed 11 changes. All 11 passed. Individual clubs proposed 15. Fewer than half survived.

Analysis of 2023–2026 NFL proposal and approval records  ·  Official NFL Operations data

Every spring, the owners of all 32 NFL teams gather for the league's annual meeting. Teams have spent the previous months drafting proposals: rules they want changed, powers they want gained, imbalances they want corrected. Most clubs arrive with at least one proposal. Most leave disappointed.

But one body at those meetings almost never leaves disappointed.

The NFL Competition Committee — an advisory group that doesn't itself own a team — proposed 11 rule changes in the four seasons between 2023 and 2026. Every single one passed. Individual clubs, with real financial stakes in the game's outcomes, proposed 15 changes across the same period. Eight passed. Seven didn't.

Understanding why those numbers are so different tells you something important about how the NFL actually works — and who actually controls it.

The Funnel

Start with the raw shape of recent rule-making. Between 2023 and 2026, the league considered 25 distinct rule change proposals. Some produced multiple votes across multiple years. At the end of the process, 18 distinct changes became permanent rules.

A 100% success rate versus a 53% rate isn't a random fluctuation. It reflects a structural reality: the Competition Committee's proposals arrive pre-negotiated. They've already been stress-tested against the interests of multiple teams, refined through internal review, and shaped by the committee's working relationships with the league office. When they reach the full owners' vote, they're not proposals anymore — they're recommendations. And recommendations from a body with no competitive interest in the outcome are much easier to adopt than proposals from a team that stands to gain from the change.

"Club proposals are advocacy. Committee proposals are conclusions."

The Detroit Exception

The Detroit Lions are the most striking case study in recent NFL advocacy. Between 2023 and 2026, Detroit submitted at least five proposals — more than any other individual club. Some passed: their push to protect a team's right to a third challenge after two successful ones was approved in 2024. Some failed: their proposal to fix playoff seeding, their proposal to remove automatic first downs for defensive penalties, their proposal to exempt injured players from the 90-man roster limit.

Detroit's pattern illustrates both the promise and the limits of club-driven rule change. Individual teams can identify real problems. They can build a case. But getting that case through the full owners' vote requires either persuading 24 of 32 teams that the change serves everyone — or having it adopted by the Competition Committee, which carries its own institutional credibility.

The most interesting signal in this dataset isn't which proposals passed. It's which ones are still failing despite recurring support. Those are the pressure points the game hasn't resolved yet.

The Committee's Role

The Competition Committee was established in the 1960s and has been shaping NFL rules ever since. It currently includes team representatives, coaches, and general managers, but its proposals reflect league-level thinking about competitive balance, player safety, and fan experience rather than any one team's interest.

That structural advantage is, in most ways, a feature rather than a bug. Rules made by a body with no direct stake in the outcome are more likely to serve the league as a whole. The kickoff redesign — one of the most consequential rule changes in recent memory — passed through the Competition Committee, not through an individual team's proposal. So did the replay authority expansions that are quietly reshaping officiating.

But it also means that teams with legitimate grievances — Detroit's complaints about automatic first downs, Green Bay's concerns about the tush push — need to either build a coalition among other clubs or somehow persuade the Competition Committee to take up their cause. Neither path is easy. And for the issues that keep failing, the gap between "real problem" and "political solution" may persist for years.
