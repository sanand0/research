# The Tyranny of a Single Letter

*In the spirit of Martin Gardner's Mathematical Games column*

---

Consider the humble word LULL. Of its four letters, three are the letter L. That's 75%—a remarkable concentration for such a common, unassuming word. Or take ASSESS, where the letter S appears four times in six letters (67%). These words exhibit what we might call "letter dominance"—a single letter asserting typographical supremacy over all others.

This raises an irresistible question: **Which English words of four or more letters contain the highest proportion of a single repeated letter?**

The puzzle seems simple enough, yet it leads us into surprising territory—touching on combinatorics, information theory, and the peculiar evolutionary pressures that shape our lexicon.

## Setting the Rules

Before we can hunt for specimens, we must establish our menagerie's boundaries. What qualifies as a "word"? For our investigation, I consulted a comprehensive dictionary of 370,105 entries, setting aside proper nouns and abbreviations. We seek only common (or at least dictionary-sanctioned) English words of four letters or more where a single letter comprises more than half of all letters present.

This ">50%" threshold eliminates many famous candidates that *feel* like they should qualify. MISSISSIPPI, that beloved example from spelling bees, contains only four I's among its eleven letters—a mere 36%. BANANA offers three A's in six letters—exactly 50%, falling just short of our criterion. Even the astronomical SYZYGY, with its exotic three Y's, achieves only 50% and thus fails to enter our collection.

## The Champions by Percentage

Our search yielded 333 qualifying words. At the summit stand two curiosities: MMMM and OOOO, each achieving the theoretical maximum of 100%. Whether these represent legitimate lexical entries or dictionary artifacts I leave to the reader's judgment.

More interesting are the four-letter words achieving 75%:

| Word | Dominant Letter | Percentage |
|------|-----------------|------------|
| LULL | L | 75% |
| SASS | S | 75% |
| EPEE | E | 75% |
| ZIZZ | Z | 75% |
| BRRR | R | 75% |

The fencer's EPEE, the onomatopoetic BRRR (for cold), and the British dialectal ZIZZ (a nap) each pack three of the same letter into four positions. Note that ZIZZ contains the rare letter Z twice—making it something of a Scrabble player's fantasy.

At six letters, we find numerous words achieving 67%:

- **ASSESS** (4 S's)
- **VOODOO** (4 O's)
- **PEEWEE** (4 E's)
- **MUUMUU** (4 U's)
- **TEEPEE** (4 E's)

These are all perfectly cromulent words that might appear in ordinary conversation—well, perhaps not ordinary, but certainly in conversation.

## The Long-Word Problem

Here lies a mathematical truth that makes our puzzle interesting: **achieving high letter concentration becomes exponentially harder as word length increases.**

In a four-letter word, three identical letters gives you 75%. But to achieve even 60% in a ten-letter word, you'd need six repetitions of a single letter—a tall order when English morphology demands variety (prefixes, suffixes, and consonant-vowel alternation).

The longest words in our collection achieving more than 50% dominance have nine letters:

- **POSSESSES** (5 S's = 56%)
- **SASSINESS** (5 S's = 56%)
- **SISSINESS** (5 S's = 56%)
- **BEEKEEPER** (5 E's = 56%)
- **ASSASSINS** (5 S's = 56%)

Notice the letter S appears in all but one of these! The sibilant's success stems from its grammatical versatility: it forms plurals (POSSESS→POSSESSES), indicates possession, and appears in many common suffixes (-NESS, -LESS). The humble S has evolved into our language's most repeatable letter.

At eight letters, we can still achieve 62.5%:

- **ASSESSES** (5 S's)
- **SWISSESS** (5 S's)

SWISSESS is a rare gem—a female person of Swiss nationality, now considered archaic. But what a specimen!

## An Information-Theoretic Digression

Claude Shannon's theory of information tells us that the "surprise" of an event is related to its improbability. Rare letters appearing repeatedly should be more surprising than common letters doing the same.

Consider: the letter E comprises about 12.7% of English text, while Z accounts for merely 0.07%. If we treat letter selection as random (a gross simplification, but instructive), seeing three E's in four letters is unlikely—but seeing two Z's in four letters is *far* more improbable.

This suggests a "dominance score" that accounts for both the proportion achieved AND the rarity of achieving it. Under such a metric, **PIZZAZZ** (57% Z) might score higher than **ASSESS** (67% S), because Z's are so much harder to come by.

Our analysis reveals that Z is approximately **8.6 times more likely** to appear as a dominant letter than its baseline frequency would predict. The letter R, by contrast, is dramatically underrepresented—suggesting that while R appears frequently in English, it rarely clusters.

## A Curious Asymmetry: Vowels vs. Consonants

Among our 333 qualifying words, the distribution reveals a striking pattern:

- **Letter E**: 89 words (27%)
- **Letter A**: 57 words (17%)
- **Letter O**: 40 words (12%)
- **Letter S**: 36 words (11%)

The vowels E, A, and O dominate the dominant letters! This makes linguistic sense: English words alternate consonants and vowels, and words from languages with simpler phonology (Hawaiian, many African languages) favor repeating vowels: MUUMUU, BOOBOO, KOODOO (variant of KUDU).

Meanwhile, some common letters rarely achieve dominance. The letter T, which comprises 9.1% of English text, dominates in only 10 of our words (3%). Similarly, R at 6% frequency produces only 3 dominant words (0.9%). These letters, it seems, prefer to be distributed rather than concentrated.

## For the Puzzle Enthusiast

I leave you with some challenges:

1. **Find a common word** (not proper noun) of 5+ letters where a consonant other than S achieves 60% dominance.

2. **The Double-Dominance Problem**: Can you find a word where *two different letters* each appear exactly the same number of times, with that count exceeding any other letter? (Example: INTESTINES has three T's and three S's... but can you find one where each comprises more than 40%?)

3. **The Constructed Word Challenge**: Using standard English word-formation rules, what's the longest word you can construct achieving 50% dominance?

4. **Proper Nouns**: If we allowed them, which names or places would top our list? MISSISSIPPI fails, but what about Hawaiian or Welsh names?

## A Final Observation

There is something deeply satisfying about words that break the expected pattern—that concentrate their essence into a single repeated symbol. LULL sounds like what it means, the L's rolling lazily. ZIZZ buzzes with sleepy sibilance. ASSASSIN hisses with menace.

Perhaps these letter-dominant words succeed not *despite* their repetition but *because of it*. They stick in memory, demanding to be noticed. In a language that prizes variety, they achieve distinction through insistent sameness.

The next time you encounter a TEEPEE or sip some COFFEE (only 50%, alas), spare a thought for the dominant letter within—that single character punching above its weight, making its presence unmistakably known.
